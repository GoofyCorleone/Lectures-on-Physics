\section{The postulates of geometrical optics and the dark Room}

        We already know the history of the geometrical optics, well before starting the study of this theory we must take back the contributions of Al Azhen and talk about the \textbf{postulates of geometrical physics} which are
    
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item The speed of light is finite.
            \item The light propagates in a straight line.
            \item The light rays obey the Euclid's postulates of plane geometry.
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item A straight line segment can be drawn joining any two points.
                \item A finite straight line (line segment) can be extended continuously in a straight line indefinitely.
                \item A circle can be drawn with any point as its center and any distance as its radius.
                \item All right angles are equal to one another, regardless of their orientation.
                
                \item If a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the interior angles on the same side less than two right angles, the two lines, if extended indefinitely, will eventually meet on that same side.
            \end{enumerate}
            \item In the matter the light's speed is less than in vacuum.
        \end{enumerate}

        Now, before starting to use these postulates we must understand the physical meaning of them. The first postulate, \textbf{The speed of light is finite} is fundamental, it is incredibly important. \\

    How could Hero of Alexandria understand that the speed of light must be finite? To be honest, I don't really know his motivation but, we could get an idea about why this should be like that from simple phenomenological reasoning without the use a mathematics or advanced physics. \textit{Lets imagine a world where the speed of light is infinite}\\

        How are going to be the nights in such world? It should be a complete chaos. Every star in the sky is a source of light, a source of infinite rays which each one travels at an infinite speed. There wont be any night, all the day is going to be full of light and even every event in the universe is going to be seen simultaneously, the big bang, the birth and death of a star. \textit{Each past and present event is going to be seen at the same time}.\\

        Other effect that only can be explained in a universe with light with finite speed. Imagine you're standing in the rain, which is falling vertically. If you start running, the rain hits you in the face. This happens because your movement changes the apparent direction of the rain. An astronomer, James Bradley, observed that the stars didn't appear to be exactly in their calculated positions. To see a star directly overhead, he had to tilt his telescope slightly in the direction of Earth's movement around the Sun, the light from the star "falls" toward Earth at a finite speed. Earth is moving. For the light beam to travel from the telescope lens to the eyepiece (and for us to see it), Earth has moved a little. Therefore, you have to point the telescope at a slight angle toward where Earth will be when the light reaches the eyepiece, not where it is at the moment it enters the lens.\\

        If the speed of light were infinite, this effect wouldn't exist. The rain (light) would fall so fast that your (Earth's) movement would be irrelevant. The fact that we have to "aim ahead" of the target is irrefutable proof that light travels at a finite speed. \\

        There are many other consequences but lets continue with all the other postulates. Now lets talk about \textbf{The light propagates in a straight line}. This does have sense because it is what is seen in any normal day, all the source of light can be seen as sources of infinite many rays which propagates at a finite speed. With tools purely geometrical there is no way to give a proof of this, however, from a wave theory for light, Huygens was able to prove that the rays are, in fact, the normal vectors of the wavefront are the rays, and, from a quantum perspective, Richard Feynman proved that the rays are the superposition of the ''watches'' that represent the probability amplitudes.\\

        Often we see that light is not an straight line, for example, an easy experiment consists on a translucid bowl full of salted water, we're going to see that the ray presents a deflection, we're going to see that this curves can be generated as a composition of infinitely many straight refractions.\\

        Because we've postulated that the light travels in straight lines, so we have to give a theory for the propagation so \textbf{The light rays obey the Euclid's postulates of plane geometry}. A point is going to be a source, a place where two rays meet, the place where two media separates, etc.\\

        The extension of a line segment to a continuously straight line is a way to make or represent ray tracing. The circle postulate talks about the sphere that propagates alongside all the rays (which behave as the radius). The right angles are just a geometrical formalism which does not have a dramatic optical interpretation.\\

        The last postulate is really important, from a physical point of view it tell us that all the rays are going to be propagate on a completely plane space, so we could ask ourselves if we want to study the light's behavior under the gravitational effects of compact objects we must change the fifth Euclid's postulate, and the short answer is yup, we have to. Under gravitational effects the differential geometry is a better tool to study the light-type observers on the curved space-time.\\

        So, we have many arguments that supports the idea the light's speed must be finite, and if it is finite there should be a way to measure it, right? The answer is yes, and here we're going to use the postulates to follow the Fizeau's ideas on how to measure the light's speed.

        In 1849, Hippolyte Fizeau performed a groundbreaking experiment that provided the first non-astronomical measurement of the speed of light. His ingenious setup transformed the problem of measuring an extremely short time interval into a problem of measuring a rotation speed, which could be done with much greater precision. Imagine a very fast runner who starts from a point $A$, runs to a point $B$ and immediately returns to $A$. To measure the runner's speed, you could place a door at $A$ that opens and closes at regular intervals. If you open the door just when the runner leaves, then close it and open it again periodically, you might catch a glimpse of him when he returns if the timing is right. But if the runner is extremely fast, the door might be closed when he gets back. This is exactly the idea Fizeau used with light, replacing the door with a rapidly rotating toothed wheel and the runner with a light pulse.\\

        Fizeau set up his apparatus on two hills in the outskirts of Paris, with the light source and observer on one hill (Montmartre) and a mirror on the other (Mont Valérien), about $8.633$ kilometers away. A beam of light from a lamp was directed toward a partially reflecting mirror (a beam splitter) that sent part of the light toward a toothed wheel. The wheel could be spun at a controlled speed and had a large number of teeth and gaps – typically $720$ teeth, meaning $720$ gaps as well. After passing through a gap, the light traveled to the distant mirror, was reflected, and returned along the same path. On its way back, it would again have to pass through a gap in the wheel to reach the observer's eye via the beam splitter.\\

        If the wheel were stationary, the returning light would pass through the same gap and be seen. But as the wheel began to rotate, the situation changed. When the wheel turned slowly, the gap through which the light had originally passed would have moved only slightly during the light's round trip, so most of the returning light still made it through. As the speed increased, the gap shifted more, and the light began to be partially blocked by the edge of a tooth. At a certain critical speed, the gap moved exactly so that by the time the light returned, the adjacent tooth had taken its place, completely eclipsing the light. The observer would see the light disappear for the first time. By knowing the rotation speed at which this first extinction occurred, the number of teeth, and the distance to the mirror, Fizeau could deduce the time it took for light to travel to the mirror and back.\\

        Let us analyze the situation mathematically. In the figure \ref{fig:Fizeau} let $d$ be the distance from the wheel to the distant mirror. The total path for the light is $2d$. The wheel has $N$ teeth and consequently $N$ gaps, so the angular width of one tooth (or one gap) is $\pi/N$ radians (since a full circle has $2\pi$ radians and there are $2N$ segments – teeth and gaps – each of equal angular size). For the first extinction, the time required for the light to make the round trip must equal the time needed for the wheel to rotate by the angle corresponding to one tooth (i.e., to replace a gap with a tooth). If the wheel rotates with angular velocity $\omega$ (radians per second), the time to turn through an angle $\pi/N$ is 
        \begin{equation}
        t_{\text{rot}} = \frac{\pi/N}{\omega}.
        \end{equation}
        The light travel time is 
        \begin{equation}
        t_{\text{light}} = \frac{2d}{c},
        \end{equation}
        and equation these gives  us
        \begin{equation}
        \frac{2d}{c} = \frac{\pi}{N \omega}.
        \end{equation}
        
        But $\omega = 2\pi f$ where $f$ is the rotation frequency in revolutions per second. Substituting,
        \begin{equation}
        \frac{2d}{c} = \frac{\pi}{N \cdot 2\pi f} = \frac{1}{2N f}.
        \end{equation}
        Solving for $c$ yields
        \begin{equation}
        c = 4 d N f.
        \end{equation}
        Fizeau used $d = 8.633 \times 10^3$ m, $N = 720$, and observed the first extinction at $f \approx 12.6$ revolutions per second. Plugging in these numbers,
        \begin{equation}
        c = 4 \times 8.633 \times 10^3 \times 720 \times 12.6 \approx 3.13 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s},
        \end{equation}
        a value about $5\%$ higher than the modern accepted value of $2.99792458 \times 10^8$ m/s, but remarkably accurate for the time. The discrepancy arose mainly from uncertainties in the distance measurement and the precise determination of the extinction condition. Fizeau's experiment was a triumph of experimental physics, demonstrating that light travels at a finite speed and providing a method that would be refined by later researchers, most notably Léon Foucault, who replaced the toothed wheel with a rotating mirror to achieve even greater precision. The conceptual beauty of Fizeau's approach lies in its translation of an infinitesimal time interval (on the order of $1/20000$ of a second) into a measurable mechanical rotation, a perfect illustration of the deep connection between optics and mechanics.\\

        Before develop mathematically the dark room we're going to set an important convention

        \begin{itemize}
            \item The light propagates from left to right along the $z$ direction.
            \item A distance measured in the direction of light is positive and a distance measured against the direction of light is negative.
        \end{itemize}

        Once defined the convention lets do it. A source in the point $A_o$ is at a distance $d_o$ respect to the opening $O$ of the dark room, ($d_o$ is in fact negative because it is measure against the light direction from the point $O$), an image if formed at a distance $d_i$ from $O$ thus we must find out a relation between these parameters and the diameter $D$ of the DR. First we're going to calculate the distances $\overline{A_oOA_i}$, $\overline{A_oRA_i}$ where $R$ is the radius of the opening, therefore

        \begin{equation}
            \overline{A_oOA_i} = d_i-d_o \;\;d_i = \overline{OA_i}\;\; d_0 = \overline{OA_o},
        \end{equation}

        \begin{equation}
            \overline{A_oRA_i} = \left[ d_i^2 + x^2 + y^2 \right]^{1/2}  - \left[ d_o^2 + x^2 + y^2 \right]^{1/2},
        \end{equation}

        having on count our convention implies that $\sqrt{d_o^2}=-d_o$ so

        \begin{equation}
            \overline{A_oRA_i} = d_i\left[1 + \frac{r^2}{d_i^2}\right]^{1/2} - d_o\left[1 + \frac{r^2}{d_o^2} \right]^{1/2}.
        \end{equation}

        Historically it was observed in the dark room that the image that was formed depended on the diameter of the opening and the distance of the object to view, it was observed that the image was formed for far objects, but how far is far? It is relative to the radius of the opening, then the ratios must satisfy that

        \begin{equation}
            \frac{r^2}{d_o^2} \ll 1\;,\;\;\frac{r^2}{d_i^2} \ll 1,
        \end{equation}

        using this and using the Taylor's theorem for a polynomial expansion of the function $\sqrt{1+x}$ then

        \begin{equation}
            \overline{A_oRA_i} \approx d_i\left[1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{r^2}{d_i^2} + \mathcal{O}(\delta^2) \right] - d_o\left[ 1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{r^2}{d_o^2} + \mathcal{O}(\delta^2) \right],
        \end{equation}

        \begin{equation}
            \overline{A_oRA_i} \approx \left(d_i-d_o\right) + \frac{r^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{d_i} - \frac{1}{d_o} \right) = \overline{A_oOA_i} + \frac{r^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{d_i} - \frac{1}{d_o} \right)
        \end{equation}

        with this we can express this in terms the difference of the light's path and the diameter of the dark room

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:PathDifferenceDR}
            \triangle L = \frac{r^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{d_i} - \frac{1}{d_o} \right) = \frac{D^2}{8}\left(\frac{1}{d_i} - \frac{1}{d_o} \right).
        \end{equation}

        In the equation \ref{eq:PathDifferenceDR} lies the hearth of the dark room, it was found by Rayleigh a certain relation between the light's wavelength and the light's path difference between the upper and lower part of the opening of the dark room, this relation (which will be discussed physically in a moment) is:

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:RayleighIneq}
            \triangle L < \frac{\lambda}{4}.
        \end{equation}

        Using this relation we will find the maximum diamater for the dark room

        \begin{equation}
             \frac{D^2}{8}\left(\frac{1}{d_i} - \frac{1}{d_o} \right) < \frac{\lambda}{4},
        \end{equation}

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:Maxdiameter}
            D_{max} = \left(\frac{2\lambda d_id_o}{d_o-d_i} \right)^{1/2}.
        \end{equation}

        In the real life there is no monochromatic light, it is forbidden by the laws of quantum mechanics, we can have quasi-monochromatic light but it is only in advanced laboratories so for a polychromatic source in the equation \ref{eq:Maxdiameter} we should do the calculations with the maximum wavelength that conforms the beam the origin of this expression have many origins which converges to the same phenomena. If we want to form an image the electromagnetic field coming from the object must arrive at the same time at the image point, if there is some kind of phase difference there will be aberrations as astigmatism or chromatic aberration (even if it is not a lens we can see it from the equation \ref{eq:Maxdiameter}) from a quantum point of view this is related to an interpretation of the \textit{size} of a photon \citep{santos2018huygens}.\\

        Now we're going to analyze how does the light interact (from a simple and geometrical point of view) with transparent media. Suppose a source radiates from a point $A$ and the ray light arrives at $B$, see figure \ref{fig:indicerefracciongeo}.

        \begin{figure}[ht]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width = 0.8\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/indicerefracciongeo.pdf}
            \caption{A schematic representation of a ray beam from $A$ to $B$ which goes through n different transparent media and in each one it had a different speed and traveled different length.}
            \label{fig:indicerefracciongeo}
        \end{figure}

        There are $n$ different transparent media each one hast a different thickness and the light in each one is different, therefore the time that takes the light to go from $A$ to $B$ is

        \begin{equation}
            t_{AB} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{L_i}{v_i} = \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{L_iC}{v_i}\frac{1}{C}\;[s].
        \end{equation}

        Then we define the refraction index in the $i$th medium as the relation between the speed of light in vacuum and the speed of light in the $i$th medium. \textit{It is a scalar in linear and homogeneous and isotropic media but mostly it is a tensor.} 

        \begin{equation}
            t_{AB} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{i=1}^nn_iL_i = \frac{L_{AB}}{C}\;[s],
        \end{equation}

        \begin{myDef}
            (Optical path): The optical path $L_{AB}$ is the distance that must travel in vacuum the light to have the same time to go from a point $A$ to a point $B$ across $n$ different media each one characterized by a different (or equal) refraction index.
        \end{myDef}

        The optical path has a length greater than the geometrical path of the light, that is why it is called optical path. This can be easily generalized to continuous media using a Lebesgue's integral (or Riemann integral if it is well behaved)

        \begin{equation}
            L_{AB} = \int_A^Bn(S)ds\;[m].
        \end{equation}

        Before going further we must have to set some definitions.

        \begin{myDef}
            (Phase): The phase is the relative angular displacement the ray has.
        \end{myDef}

        \begin{myDef}
            (Wavefront): The geometrical space where the phase of the light is constant is called the wavefront of the light.
        \end{myDef}

    \section{The Snell-Descartes law and the Fermat's principle }

        As we wad discussed in the historical chapter it was Heron of Alexandria who realizes that in light reflection it travels the least distance and Al Azhen proposes a maximum time, it was Fermat who formalizes it as a principle the \textbf{Fermat's principle}

        \begin{myPrinciple}
            (Fermat's principle): The light travels a trajectory such that the optical path is stationary

            \begin{equation}
                \delta L = 0.
            \end{equation}
        \end{myPrinciple}

        The Fermat's principle can be ``deduced¨ from the Hamilton principle or even more from a variational principle, lets give a look to it, lets look for an stationary curve for the time the light travels

        \begin{equation}
            t = \int_{t_0}^{t_f}dt = \int_{A}^B\frac{1}{C}dL = \int_{\tau_0}^{\tau_f}Ld\tau,
        \end{equation}

        from here we see that, in a mathematical way, if we want to find the relation between the trajectory of the light and the time it takes to go from $A$ to $B$ we will have to solve the Euler-Lagrange equation. Now lets use the Fermat's principle to see how the light interacts with a diopter (a refracting surface) in a vector way, known as the \textit{vectorial Snell-Descartes law}.\\

        \begin{myDef}
            \textit{(Diopter)}: A Diopter is a surface $\Sigma \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ such that it separates two media with different refraction index.
        \end{myDef}

        \begin{figure}[ht]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width = 0.9\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/SnellDescartes.pdf}
            \caption{two media separated by a diopter which an observer $O$ measures the path the light will follow from $A$ to $B$ and proposes a displacement of the source $A$ to $A'$ to see how must be the light path.}
            \label{fig:SnellDescartes}
        \end{figure}

        In the figure \ref{fig:SnellDescartes} we see two media separated by a transparent surface (diopter) with refractive index $n_o$,$n_1$, respectively, a source $A$ emits a beam which reaches the diopter in $I$ and it is refracted to $B$, from $A$ there is an unitary vector $\hat{u}_o$ in  the direction $\overline{AI}$ and the observer $O$ analyzes a variation of $A$ as $A'$ and the incident point on the diopter is $I'$, then lets analyze it vectorially

        \begin{align}
            L = \left|\left| \Vec{I} - \Vec{A} \right|\right|,\\
            \triangle\Vec{I} = \Vec{I}'-\Vec{I},\\
            \triangle\Vec{A} = \Vec{A}'-\Vec{A},
        \end{align}

        with this we can calculate the path difference $L - L'$ vectorially as a proyection of the unitary vector in the direction of $L$ given by $\hat{u}_o$ along $\triangle\Vec{I}-\triangle\Vec{A}$ 

        \begin{equation}
            \triangle L_A = \frac{\left(\Vec{I} - \Vec{A} \right)\cdot\left(\triangle \Vec{I} - \triangle \Vec{A} \right)}{\left|\left|\Vec{I} - \Vec{A} \right|\right|} = \hat{u}_o \cdot \left(\triangle\Vec{I}-\triangle\Vec{A} \right),
        \end{equation}

        now for the optical path

        \begin{equation}
            \mathcal{L}_A = n_o\triangle L = n_o\hat{u}_o \left(\triangle\Vec{I}-\triangle\Vec{A} \right),
        \end{equation}

        doing the same for the right side of the diopter but for the point $B$ we then have got the total optical path difference as

        \begin{equation}
            \triangle\mathcal{L} = n_o\hat{u}_o\cdot\left(\triangle\Vec{I}-\triangle\Vec{A} \right) + n_1\hat{u}_1\cdot\left(\triangle\Vec{B}-\triangle\Vec{I} \right)
        \end{equation}

        because the points $A$ and $B$ are fixed then $\triangle\Vec{A}=0$ and $\triangle\Vec{B}=0$ thus

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:PathDifferenceSD}
            \triangle\mathcal{L} = \left(n_o\hat{u_o}-n_1\hat{u}_1\right)\cdot\triangle\Vec{I}.
        \end{equation}

        If the Fermat's principle is satisfied the equation \ref{eq:PathDifferenceSD} implies straightfully that $\triangle\Vec{I}$ is a tangent vector to the diopter this implies then

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:SnellDescartes}
            n_o\hat{u_o}-n_1\hat{u_1} = k\hat{N}.
        \end{equation}

        The equation \ref{eq:SnellDescartes} is known as the vectorial Snell-Descartes law, from this equation we can arrive to the law of reflection and refraction to do it we only have to do the cross product of the equation \ref{eq:SnellDescartes}

        \begin{equation}
            \hat{N}\times \left(n_0\hat{u_0}-n_1\hat{u_1} \right) = k \hat{N}\times\hat{N}=0,
        \end{equation}

        \begin{equation}
            n_0sin(\theta_0) = n_1sin(\theta_1).
        \end{equation}

    \section{The Descartes's ovoid and diopters}

        From here on we are going to start to study the theory of image formation where the concepts of \textbf{object} and \textbf{image} are really important because there are real and virtual objects and image, this depends on the relation between these points ant the diopter, in fact, something is called virtual if we put a photo detector in that point and we wont measure any field if an object is to the left of a diopter is called real and if it is to the right is called virtual, in the same order of ideas if the image is to the left of the diopter it is virtual and if it is to the right is real.\\

        Before starting we have to give some fundamental definitions

        \begin{myDef}
            \textit{(Optical system)} an optical system is a set of translucid media (diopters) and reflective media (catopters) and we say that the \textbf{system is centered} if all the diopters are revolution surfaces and shared the same symmetry axis.

            The systems conformed only by refractive surfaces all called \textbf{dioptric systems} and the system conformed only by reflective surfaces are called \textbf{catoptric systems} and the systems formed by these two kind of surfaces are called \textbf{catadioptrics systems}.
        \end{myDef}

        \begin{myDef}
            \textit{(Real and virtual points)} Given a optical system $\mathcal{S}$ we say that an object point $A_o$ is real if it is to the left and outside the optical system and we way that the object point is virtual if it is to the right and inside the optical system.

            In the same way, we say that an image point $A_i$ is real if it is to the right and outside the optical system, and we way that the image point is virtual if it is inside the optical system.
        \end{myDef}

        \begin{myDef}
            \textit{(Stigmatism)} From a \textbf{geometrical point of view} we say that an optical system $\mathcal{S}$ is \textbf{stigmatic} if for any given point source, all the rays that pass through $\mathcal{S}$ converge to a point source. In other words, a optical system $\mathcal{S}$ is stigmatic if for any  object point there is one unique image point.\\

            We can also, define the stigmatism from a wavefront perspective. An rigorously stigmatic system is one that transforms an spherical wavefront $\mathcal{A}$ to a new spherical wavefront $\mathcal{A}'$ whose curvature centers, $A_o$ and $A_i$ are the rigorously stigmatic points (see figure \ref{fig:Stigmatism} ). Thus, the spherical wavefront $\mathcal{A}'$ is the image of the spherical wavefront $\mathcal{A}$.
        \end{myDef}

        Understanding that an image-forming optical system is one that maps each point source of an object's electromagnetic field to the point sources of the image's electromagnetic field, corresponding to a homothety between these fields, the interest arises in studying what these optical systems are and what their characteristics are. In general, these systems can be composed of multiple elements, but initially we will study the simplest configuration for two reasons:

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item because it most easily allows us to understand the principles of these systems.
            \item because later, more complex systems can be understood as compositions of these.
        \end{enumerate}
        These elementary systems are made up of a single optical surface, either refractive or reflective, which is capable of perfectly focusing all the rays emitted by a point source into a perfect point image. In geometric optics, this means that all the rays travel the same optical path length. Initially, this property associated with a single pair of points (stigmatism) will be studied, and then the conditions under which this property becomes invariant to the translation of these points, which is known as aplanetism, will be studied.\\
 
        Now we are going to develope the \textit{rigurous stigmatism} thes are system capable of making perfect images, this means from a point source there is a perfect point image. Let a diopter which separates two media with refraction index $n_o$ and $n_i$ to the left of the diopter there is an object $A_o$ at a point $[0,0,z_o]$ the field from $A_o$ reaches a point $I$, measured from the diopter's vertex $v$, from the point I the beam is refracted and forms an image $A_i$ at a point $[0,0,z_i]$ then we are going to find the relations between all these parameters to make all the rays from $A_o$ arrive at $A_i$ making a perfect image. From the Fermat's principle we have got

        \begin{equation}
            n_0l_0 + n_il_i = cte
        \end{equation}

        \begin{figure}[ht]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/AlmostOvoid.pdf}
            \caption{Scheme representation of the development of a rigorously stigmatic system, from a point source $A_o$ at a position $[0,0,z_o]$ the diopter with a vertice $v$ forms a perfect image $A_i$ at $[0,0,z_i]$. }
            \label{fig:AlmostOvoid}
        \end{figure}

        the, as we can see in the figure \ref{fig:AlmostOvoid} we then have

        \begin{equation}
            -n_0[x^2+y^2 + (z-z_o)^2]^{1/2} + n_i[x^2+y^2 + (z-z_i)^2]^{1/2} = cte
        \end{equation}

        and, remembering our sign convention $\sqrt{z_o^2}=-zo$, $z_i^2=z_i$ and to find the constant we can see that if we want to make all the rays from $A_o$ arrive to $A_i$ then the rays that travel parallel to $z$ satisfy also the Fermat's principle, thus

        \begin{equation}
            n_iz_i-n_oz_o = cte,
        \end{equation}

        \begin{equation}
            n_i[x^2+y^2 + (z-z_i)^2]^{1/2} -n_0[x^2+y^2 + (z-z_o)^2]^{1/2}  = n_iz_i-n_oz_o,
        \end{equation}
        now, we are going to do the following change of variables given by 

        \begin{equation}
            \rho^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2,
        \end{equation}
        which represents the square of the vector from the vertex to the interface shown in \ref{fig:AlmostOvoid}, so, having this on count, the equation  becomes
        
        \begin{align}\label{eq:Ovoid1}
            & n_i\left[\rho^2 +z_i^2 -2zz_i\right]^{1/2}- n_0\left[ \rho^2 +z_0^2 -2zz_0 \right]^{1/2} = n_iz_i - n_0z_0.
        \end{align}        

        Now, the main goal is to transform the equation \ref{eq:Ovoid1} into a more explicit and useful algebraic equation, so, the first thing we're going to do is to square both side of the equation \ref{eq:Ovoid1} and then we'll have

        \begin{equation*}
            n_o^2 \left[ \rho^2 + z_o^2 - 2zz_o \right] + n_i^2\left[  \rho^2 + z_i - 2zz_i\right] - 2n_on_1 \left[ \rho^2 + z_o^2 -2zz_o \right]^{1/2}\left[\rho^2 + z_i^2 - 2zz_i\right]^{1/2} = \left( n_iz_i - n_oz_o \right)^2
        \end{equation*}

        Now, we're going to re-order all the equation for convenience as follows

        \begin{equation*}
            n_o^2 \left[ \rho^2 + z_o^2 - 2zz_o \right] + n_i^2\left[  \rho^2 + z_i - 2zz_i\right] - \left( n_iz_i - n_oz_o \right)^2 = 2n_on_1 \left[ \rho^2 + z_o^2 -2zz_o \right]^{1/2}\left[\rho^2 + z_i^2 - 2zz_i\right]^{1/2},
        \end{equation*}
        on the left hand side we have got the following
        \begin{align*}
            & n_o^2 \left[ \rho^2 + z_o^2 - 2zz_o \right] + n_i^2\left[  \rho^2 + z_i - 2zz_i\right] - \left( n_iz_i - n_oz_o \right)^2 = \\
            & n_o^2 \left[ \rho^2 + z_o^2 - 2zz_o \right] + n_i^2\left[  \rho^2 + z_i - 2zz_i\right] - \left( n_i^2z_i^2 + n_o^2z_o^2 - 2n_on_iz_oz_i \right) = \\
            & n_o^2\left[ \rho^2 - 2zz_o \right] + n_i^2\left[ \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right] + 2n_on_iz_oz_i,
        \end{align*}
        where we just only simplified the common terms. Having this on count, the equation reduces to

        \begin{equation}
            n_o^2\left[ \rho^2 - 2zz_o \right] + n_i^2\left[ \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right] + 2n_on_iz_oz_i = 2n_on_1 \left[ \rho^2 + z_o^2 -2zz_o \right]^{1/2}\left[\rho^2 + z_i^2 - 2zz_i\right]^{1/2}.
        \end{equation}

        Now, we must square again, the whole equation as follows
        \begin{align*}
            & \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o \right) + n_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_i \right) \right]^2 +\left( 2n_on_iz_oz_i \right)^2 + 4n_on_iz_oz_i \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right) + n_i^2\left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) \right] = \\
            & 4n_o^2n_i^2\left( \rho^2 + z_o^2 - 2zz_o \right)\left( \rho^2 + z_i^2 - 2zz_i \right),
        \end{align*}
        now we are going to expand the product on the right hand side in the following way

        \begin{align*}
            & \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o \right) + n_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_i \right) \right]^2 +\left( 2n_on_iz_oz_i \right)^2 + 4n_on_iz_oz_i \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right) + n_i^2\left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) \right] = \\
            & \text{\color{red} $4n_o^2n_i^2\left( \rho^2 - 2zz_o \right)\left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right)$} + 4n_o^2n_i^2 \left[ z_o^2 \left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) + z_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right)  +z_o^2z_i^2 \right].
        \end{align*}

        Now, we must do a simplification, to do this, we must expand the first term of the left hand side like this
        \begin{equation*}
            \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o \right) + n_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_i \right) \right]^2 = \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right)\right]^2 + \left[ n_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_i \right)\right]^2 + \text{\color{red} $ 2n_o^2n_i^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o \right)\left( \rho^2 -2zz_i \right) $ },
        \end{equation*}
        so the first term of the left hand side have on common in its expansion the same term than the expansion of the term on the right hand side, so, we could simplify then and look at the binomial is the same but with a minus sign instead of a plus sign, thus

        \begin{align*}
            & \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o \right) - n_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_i \right) \right]^2 +\left( 2n_on_iz_oz_i \right)^2 + 4n_on_iz_oz_i \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right) + n_i^2\left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) \right] = \\
            & 4n_o^2n_i^2 \left[ z_o^2 \left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) + z_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right)  +z_o^2z_i^2 \right].
        \end{align*}

        Now, we are going to forget, momentarily the first term of the left hand side and focus on all the rest of the equations because there are going to be many important simplifications. Then, having this on count we'll have the following:

        \begin{align*}
            & \left( 2n_on_iz_oz_i \right)^2 + 4n_on_iz_oz_i \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right) + n_i^2\left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) \right] - 4n_o^2n_i^2 \left[ z_o^2 \left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) + z_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right)  +z_o^2z_i^2 \right] = 0
        \end{align*}
        \begin{align*}
            & 4n_on_i \left[ z_oz_in_o^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o\right) + z_oz_in_i^2\left(\rho^2 - 2zz_i  \right) -n_on_iz_o^2 \left( \rho^2 -2zz_i \right) - n_on_iz_i^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o \right)  \right.\\
            & \left.  - n_on_izo^2z_i^2 + n_on_iz_o^2z_i^2\right],
        \end{align*}
        now, we have to factorize the common factors
        \begin{align*}
            & 4n_on_i\left[ \left( \rho^2-2zz_o \right)\left( z_oz_in_o^2 - n_on_iz_i^2 \right) + \left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right)\left( z_oz_in_i^2-n_on_iz_o^2 \right) \right] = \\
        & 4n_on_i\left[ z_in_o \left( \rho2 - 2zz_o \right)\left( z_on_o - n_iz_i \right) + z_on_i \left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right)\left( z_in_i - z_on_o \right) \right] = \\
        & 4n_on_i \left[ n_iz_i - n_oz_o \right]\left[ n_iz_o \left( \rho^2 - 2zz_i \right) - n_oz_i \left( \rho^2 - 2zz_o \right) \right]=\\
        & 4n_on_i \left[ n_iz_i-n_oz_o \right]\left[ \rho^2\left( n_iz_o - n_oz_i \right) + 2zz_iz_o \left(n_o-n_i\right) \right].
        \end{align*}

        So, joining this result with the first term we will have the following

        \begin{equation*}
            \left[ n_o^2\left( \rho^2 -2zz_o \right) - n_i^2\left( \rho^2-2zz_i \right) \right]^2 +  4n_on_i \left[ n_iz_i-n_oz_o \right]\left[ \rho^2\left( n_iz_o - n_oz_i \right) + 2zz_iz_o \left(n_o-n_i\right) \right] = 0,
        \end{equation*}
        so, the last things we have to do are is re organize in the following way

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:DescartesOvoid}
            \left[ \left(n_i^2-n_o^2\right)\rho^2 - 2z\left( n_o^2z_o - n_i^2z_i  \right) \right]^2 - 4n_in_o \left[ n_iz_i - n_oz_o \right]\left[ \left( n_oz_i - n_iz_o \right)\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_i\left(n_i - n_o\right) \right]=0.
        \end{equation}

        \textbf{\textit{The equation \ref{eq:DescartesOvoid} is the algebraic expression for the Descartes Ovoid the rigorously stigmatic surface we wanted.}} 

    \section{The spherical diopter}

        Now, we are going to see that the Descartes Ovoid \ref{eq:DescartesOvoid} contains the information of all the other diopters known. So, the first one we are going to deduce is the spherical diopter this is obtained in a vicinity of the vertex shown in the figure \ref{fig:AlmostOvoid}. Therefore, to deduce this particular case, we are going to take the equation \ref{eq:DescartesOvoid} and rewrite in the following convenient way

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:DescartesOvoid2}
            \left[ \left(n_o^2-n_i^2\right)\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_i\left( \frac{n_i^2}{z_o} - \frac{n_o^2}{z_i}\right) \right]^2 - 4n_in_o \left[ n_iz_i - n_oz_o \right]\left[ \left( n_oz_i - z_iz_o \right)\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_i\left(n_i - n_o\right) \right]=0.
        \end{equation}

        So, the main idea is to construct the sphere that is located in the vertex $v$. To do this, we must see that the squared term in \ref{eq:DescartesOvoid2} looks very similar to the right factor term in the second term, so, we want to equate them and then simplify, which leads us to the following equations

        \begin{align}
            & n_o^2 - n_i^2 = c\left( n_oz_i - n_iz_o \right)\label{eq:1},\\
            & \frac{n_i^2}{z_o} - \frac{n_o^2}{z_i} = c\left(n_i - n_o\right)\label{eq:2}
        \end{align}
        where $c$ is a constant we must calculate. The second thing we are going to do, is to re-order the equation \ref{eq:1} in the following way
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:3}
            n_o^2 - cz_in_o = n_i^2 - cz_on_i,
        \end{equation}
        and if we re-order the equation \ref{eq:2} we'll have
        \begin{align*}
            &z_in_i^2 - z_on_o^2 = cz_oz_i(n_i-n_o),\\
            &z_in_i^2 - cz_oz_in_i = z_on_o^2 - cz_oz_in_o,
        \end{align*}
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:4}
            z_o\left( n_o^2 - cz_in_o \right) = z_i \left( n_i^2 - cz_on_i \right).
        \end{equation}

        So if we use \ref{eq:3} on \ref{eq:4} we have got the following two equations
        \begin{align}
            & \left( z_o-z_i \right)\left( n_i^2 - cz_on_i \right) = 0 \label{eq:5},\\
            & \left( z_o-z_i \right)\left( n_o^2 - cz_in_o \right) = 0 \label{eq:6},
        \end{align}
        using the equations \ref{eq:5} and \ref{eq:6} we will have
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:Case1}
            z_o = \frac{n_i}{c}, \qquad z_i = \frac{n_0}{c},
        \end{equation}
        and using the equation \ref{eq:1} we will have
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:Case2}
            z_0 = z_i \qquad z_{0,i} = \frac{n_o+n_i}{c}.
        \end{equation}

        Now, before going further analyzing this results, we have to use the equations \ref{eq:1}, \ref{eq:2} on \ref{eq:DescartesOvoid2}

        \begin{align*}
        & \left[ c\left( n_oz_i - n_iz_o \right)\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_ic(n_i-n_o) \right]^2 - 4n_in_o \left[ n_iz_i - n_oz_o \right]\left[ \left( n_oz_i - z_iz_o \right)\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_i\left(n_o - n_o\right) \right]=0,\\
        & \left[\left( n_oz_i - n_iz_o \right)c\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_ic(n_i-n_o) \right]\left[ \left( n_oz_i - n_iz_o \right)\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_i(n_i-n_o) - 4n_in_o\left(n_iz_i-n_oz_o\right) \right] = 0,
        \end{align*}
        and by the fundamental theorem of Algebra, we have got the equation for the spherical diopter
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:SphericalDiopter}
            \left( n_oz_i - n_iz_o \right)\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_i(n_i-n_o) = 0.
        \end{equation}

        \subsection{The Young-Weirstrass points}

            From the case \ref{eq:Case1} we have that
            \begin{equation*}
                n_iz_i - n_oz_o=0,
            \end{equation*}
            and using the equation \ref{eq:SphericalDiopter} we will have
            \begin{equation}
                \rho^2\left( n_o^2-n_i^2\right) + 2czz_on_i(n_i-n_o) = 0,
            \end{equation}
            now, dividing by $n_o^2-n_i^2$ and then we must complete squares as follows

            \begin{equation*}
                x^2+y^2 + z^2 + \frac{2czz_oz_i}{n_i+n_o} = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + \frac{2czz_oz_i}{n_i+n_o} + \text{\color{red} $\left( \frac{cz_oz_i}{n_i+n_o} \right)^2$}
                = \text{\color{red} $\left( \frac{cz_oz_i}{n_i+n_o} \right)^2$},
            \end{equation*}

            \begin{equation}\label{eq:SphericalDiopter1}
                x^2 + y^2 + \left(z - c\frac{z_oz_i}{n_o+n_i}\right)^2 = c^2 \frac{z_o^2z_i^2}{(n_i+n_o)^2},
            \end{equation}
            which corresponds to the equation of an sphere whose radius is given by
            \begin{equation}
                R = c \frac{z_oz_i}{n_o+n_i} \longrightarrow x^2 + y^2 + \left(z-R\right)^2 = R^2,
            \end{equation}
            and using \ref{eq:Case1} $z_o=n_i/c,\;z_i=n_o/c$ we will have

            \begin{align}
                & R = c \frac{z_in_i}{c(n_o+n_i)} \longrightarrow z_i = R\left(1 + \frac{n_o}{n_i}\right),\\
                & R = c \frac{z_on_o}{c(n_o+n_i)} \longrightarrow z_i = R\left(1 + \frac{n_i}{n_o}\right)
            \end{align}
            which are known as \textbf{Young-Weirstrass} points or the aplanetic points. One of these points is inside and the other outside the sphere as is shown in \ref{fig:YoungWeirstrass}.

            \begin{figure}[ht]
                \centering
                \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/YoungWeirstrass.pdf}
                \caption{The spherical Diopter for the first case, where the object point is real and it is outside the sphere but the image point is virtual and inside the diopter.}
                \label{fig:YoungWeirstrass}
            \end{figure}

        \subsection{The second spherical diopter}

            So, for the second case we have \ref{eq:Case2} and the equation \ref{eq:SphericalDiopter} then let $z_c$ given by
            \begin{equation*}
                z_c=z_o=z_i = \frac{n_i+n_o}{c},
            \end{equation*}
            then we will have 

            \begin{align}
                & \rho^2 \left( n_oz_c - n_iz_c \right) + 2zz_c^2 (n_i-n_o) = 0,\\
                & \rho^2 - 2zz_c=0,
            \end{align}
            again we must complete squares in the following way
            \begin{align}
                & x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 2zz_c + \text{\color{red}$z_c^2$} = \text{\color{red} $z_c^2$},\\
                & x^2 + y^2 + (z-z_c)^2 = z_c^2\label{eq:SphericalDiopter2}.
            \end{align}

            The equation \ref{eq:SphericalDiopter2} is an spherical diopter where the object point is on the center and the image point is also on the center.

        \section{Objects and images to the infinity}

            Some other cases arise when either the object or the image is at infinity, which is a convenient condition when the object is very far away, for example, in telescopes.

            But even in microscopy, systems are designed to form the image at infinity, since under these conditions, an eyepiece facilitates visual accommodation for observation.

            For an object in the infinity we divide by $z_o^2$ and take the limit $z_o\to -\infty$ in \ref{eq:DescartesOvoid} in the following way

            \begin{align*}
                & \lim_{z_o\to-\infty} \left[ \frac{\rho^2 \left( n_o^2-n_i^2 \right)}{z_o} + 2z \frac{n_o^2z_o + n_i^2z_i}{z_o} \right]^2 - \lim_{z_o\to-\infty} \frac{4n_on_i \left( n_oz_o + n_iz_i \right)}{z_o}\left[ \frac{\rho^2 \left( n_oz_i + n_iz_o \right)}{z_o} \right.\\
                & \left. - \frac{2zz_oz_i(n_i-n_o)}{z_o} \right] = 0,\\
                & (2zn_o^2)^2 - 4n_o2n_i^2\left[ \rho^2 n_i - 2zz_i(n_i-n_o) \right] = 0,\\
                & 4z^2n_o^4 - 4n_o^2n_i^2\rho^2 + 8zz_in_o^2n_i^2 - 8zz_in_o^3n_i = 0, \qquad \text{Dividing all the equation by $4n_o^2$},\\
                & z^2\left( n_o^2-n_i^2 \right) + 2zn_iz_i(n_i-n_o) - n_i (x^2+y^2) = 0,\\
                & \left[ n_o-n_i \right] \left[ z^2\left( n_o+n_i \right) - 2zn_iz_i \right] - n_i(x^2+y^2) = 0.
            \end{align*}

            Now, to complete the algebraic expression we must multiply all the equation by $(n_o+n_i)$ and complete the squares as follows 

            \begin{align*}
                & (n_o-n_i)\left[(n_o+n_i)\text{\color{red}$(n_o+n_i)$}  z^2 - 2zn_iz_i \text{\color{red}$(n_o+n_i)$}\right] - n_i^2 \text{\color{red}$(n_o+n_i)$} (x^2+y^2) = 0,\\
                & (n_o-n_i)\left[ (n_o+n_i)^2z^2 - 2zn_iz_i(n_o+n_i) + \text{\color{red} $n_i^2z_i^2$} - n_i^2(n_o+n_i)(x^2+y^2) \right] = \text{\color{red} $(n_o-n_i)n_i^2z_i^2$ },\\
                & (n_o-n_i) \left[(n_o+n_i)z-n_iz_i\right]^2 - n_i^2(n_o+n_i)(x^2+y^2) = (n_o-n_i)n_i^2z_i^2
            \end{align*}
            and dividing all by $(n_o-n_i)n_i^2z_i^2$ we will have

            \begin{equation}\label{eq:ConicalDiopter}
                \left( \frac{n_i+n_o}{n_iz_i} \right)^2 \left[ z - \frac{n_iz_i}{n_i+n_o} \right]^2 + \frac{n_i+n_o}{z_i^2(n_i-n_o)}(x^2+y^2) = 1.
            \end{equation}

            The result \ref{eq:ConicalDiopter} represents a conical section of revolution around the z-axis from this equation we are going to obtain the Ellipsoid and the hyperboloid.

            \subsection{Revolution Ellipsoid}

                if $n_i>n_o$ we are going to define the following scalars

                \begin{equation}
                    a = \left( \frac{n_i-n_o}{n_i+n_o} \right)^{1/2}|z_i|, \qquad b = \left( \frac{n_i}{n_i+n_o} \right)|z_i|,
                \end{equation}
                and notice that $b>a$ so we will have \textbf{prolate ellipsoid} (see figure \ref{fig:Ellipsoid} given by
                \begin{equation}
                    \frac{1}{b^2}\left(z-b\right)^2 + \frac{1}{a^2}(x^2+y^2) = 1.
                \end{equation}

                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/Ellipsoid.pdf}
                    \caption{Prolate Ellipsoid centered and $z=B$.}
                    \label{fig:Ellipsoid}
                \end{figure}

                We have got two cases, when $z_i > 0$ and $z_i< 0$. For both cases what is next is to study the eccentricity of the ellipse and the foci of the ellipsoids.

                \textbf{When $z_i>0$} lets define the segment $c = \overline{F_1B}$ such that the eccentricty is given by

                \begin{equation}
                    \epsilon = \frac{c}{b}= \frac{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}{b} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{a^2}{b^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{n_o^2}{n_i^2}} = \frac{n_o}{n_i}<1,
                \end{equation}
                therefore the foci $F_1$ is centered to a distance
                \begin{equation}
                    f_1 = b-c = b-\epsilon b = (1-\epsilon)b = \frac{n_i-n_o}{n_i+n_o}z_i,
                \end{equation}
                and the second foci is at
                \begin{equation}
                    f_2 = b+c = b+\epsilon b = (1+\epsilon)b = z_i.
                \end{equation}

                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/Ellipsoid2.pdf}
                    \caption{Ellipsoid diopter when $n_i > n_o$.}
                    \label{fig:Ellipsoid2}
                \end{figure}

                This means, that for this first case that all the rays that comes parallel to the $z$-axis from infinity converges to the second Foci of the ellipsoid.\\

                If $z_i<0$ by the convention we have that $\sqrt{z_i^2} = -z_i$ and we define 

                \begin{equation}
                    b = - \frac{n_i}{n_i+n_o} z_i,
                \end{equation}
                then the conical section equation \ref{eq:ConicalDiopter} becomes 

                \begin{equation}
                    \frac{1}{b^2}(z+b)^2 + \frac{1}{a^2}(x^2+y^2) = 1.
                \end{equation}

                Again, we define $c= \overline{F_1B}$ so the eccentricty is given by

                \begin{equation*}
                    \epsilon = \frac{c}{b}\frac{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}{b} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{a^2}{b^2}} = \frac{n_o}{n_i}<1,
                \end{equation*}
                and the foci is now going to be to a distance
                \begin{equation}
                    f_1 = -b -c = -b - \epsilon b = -b (1+\epsilon) = z_i,
                \end{equation}
                and the second one is at
                \begin{equation}
                    f_2 = -b+c = -b + \epsilon b = (\epsilon-1)b = \frac{n_i-n_o}{n_i+n_o}z_i.
                \end{equation}

                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/Ellipsoid3.pdf}
                    \caption{Ellipsoid diopter when $n_i>n_o$ and $z_i < 0$.}
                    \label{fig:Ellipsoid3}
                \end{figure}

                Having all these results on count, we can see in figure \ref{fig:Ellipsoid3} that all the rays that becomes parallel to the $z$-axis and from infinity diverges as they would come from a point source from $F_1$.
            \subsection{Revolution Hyperboloid}

                In the case $n_i < n_o$ we have got that the equation \ref{eq:ConicalDiopter} becomes an hyperboloid and, again, there are two possibilities, $z_i<0,z_i>0$, so the conical equation becomes

                \begin{equation}
                    \left( \frac{n_i+no}{n_iz_i} \right)^2\left[ z - \frac{n_iz_i}{n_i+n_o} \right] - \frac{n_i+n_o}{z_i^2(n_o-n_i)}(x^2+y^2) = 1.
                \end{equation}

                If $z_i > 0$ we define again the following scalars
                \begin{align*}
                    & b^2 = \frac{n_i^2}{(n_i+n_o)^2}z_i^2,\\
                    & a^2 = \frac{n_o-n_i}{(n_i+n_o)^2}z_i^2,
                \end{align*}
                so the equation of the hyperboloid (see figure \ref{fig:Hyperboloid}) becomes

                \begin{equation}
                    \frac{1}{b^2}(z-b)^2 - \frac{1}{a^2}(x^2+y^2) = 1.
                \end{equation}

                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/Hyperboloid.pdf}
                    \caption{Geometrical representation of the two leaf hyperboloid with the parameters $a,b$ and foci $F_1$ and $F_2$.}
                    \label{fig:Hyperboloid}
                \end{figure}

                Again, to give a characterization of the conical surface we are going to define the segment $c = \overline{F_1B}$ such that the eccentricity is given by
                
                \begin{equation*}
                    \epsilon = \frac{c}{b} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}{b} = \sqrt{1+\frac{a^2}{b^2}} = \frac{n_o}{n_i} > 1.
                \end{equation*}

                Thus the two Foci $F_1$ and $F_2$ are located at the distances
                \begin{align}
                    & f_1 = b-c = b-\epsilon b = (1-\epsilon)b = \frac{n_i-n_o}{n_o+n_i}z_o,\\
                    &f_2 = b+c = b + \epsilon b = (1+\epsilon) b = z_i.
                \end{align}

                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/Hyperboloid2.pdf}
                    \caption{Geometrical representation of the first case of a two leaf hyperboloid. }
                    \label{fig:Hyperboloid2}
                \end{figure}

                In the figure \ref{fig:Hyperboloid2} we can see the geometrical behavior of this first case, all the rays comes from the infinity and converges to a point which is located in the second foci $F_2$.\\

                Now, for the second case, if $z_i < 0$ we define 
                \begin{equation*}
                    b= - \frac{n_i}{n_i+n_o}z_o,
                \end{equation*}
                in such way that $b > 0$ with this we write
                \begin{equation*}
                    \frac{1}{b^2}(z+b)^2 - \frac{1}{a^2}(x^2+y^2) = 1.
                \end{equation*}

                Again, we define the segment $c = \overline{F_1B}$ such that the eccentricity is given by
                \begin{equation*}
                    \epsilon = \frac{c}{b}= \frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}{b} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{a^2}{b^2}} = \frac{n_o}{n_i}>1. 
                \end{equation*}

                As we can see in the figure \ref{fig:Hyperboloid3} all the rays come from the infinity and interact with the hyperboloid diopter an the rays refracted in such geometrical way that they behave as they come from a object point source located in the foci of the first leaf at $F_1$.
                
        \section{Rigurously stigmatic surfaces by reflection}
                
                The reflective surfaces are the main components in the morden giant telescopes, for example, the James Webb telecopes is an array of many reflective surfaces which are electronically displaced such that the image formation gets the least aberration possible. Also, the reflective surfaces are better than the refractive ones in certain cases, because the optical systems can be smaller and don't have chromatic aberrations.

                Tradionally, the reflective surface are developed indepentdenly from the refractive surface. Under this formulations, the rigurously stigmatic surfaces by reflection can be obtained doing $n_i = -n_o$, where the Descartes ovals become a conic surface. To this case, the equation \ref{eq:DescartesOvoid} becomes
                \begin{align*}
                    & \left\{ \left[\left(-n_o\right)^2-n_o^2 \right]\rho^2 - 2z\left[\left( n_o^2z_o- (-n_o\right)^2z_i  \right] \right\}^2 \\
                    & -4(-n_o)n_o\left[(-n_o)z_i -n_oz_o \right]\left\{\left[n_oz_i - (-n_o)z_o \right]\rho^2 + 2zz_oz_i\left[(-n_o) - n_o \right]  \right\}=0,
                \end{align*}
                simplyfing we've got
                \begin{align*}
                    & \left\{ -2zn_o^2\left[z_o-z_i\right] \right\}^2 -4n_o^3\left[z_i+z_o\right]\left\{n_o\left[z_i+z_o \right]\rho^2 - 4zz_oz_in_o \right\} = 0,\\
                    & 4z^2n_o^4\left(z_o-z_i\right)^2 - 4n_o^4\left(z_o+z_i\right)^2\rho^2 + 16n_o^4zz_oz_i\left(z_o+z_i\right) = 0,
                \end{align*}
                dividing all by $-16n_o^4z_oz_i$ an having on count that $\rho^2 = x^2+y^2+z^2$ we've got
                \begin{align*}
                    & \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4z_oz_i}\left(x^2+y^2\right) + \frac{(z_o-z_i)^2}{4z_oz_i}z^2 - \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4z_oz_i}z^2 - z(z_o+z_i) = 0,\\
                    & \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4z_oz_i}\left(x^2+y^2\right) +z^2 - z(z_o+z_i) = 0,
                \end{align*}
                and completing the squares we've got
                \begin{equation}\label{eq:ReflectionConic}
                    \left(z - \frac{z_o+z_i}{2}\right)^2 + \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4z_oz_i}\left(x^2+y^2\right) = \left(\frac{z_o+z_i}{2}\right)^2,
                \end{equation}
                which is precisely the equation of a conic of revolution around the $z$ axis, exactly as it happened with the refractive surfaces, only that now the two indices have collapsed into a single reflective condition. The whole family of stigmatic mirrors is hidden inside \ref{eq:ReflectionConic}, and the sign of the product $z_oz_i$ is what decides which conic we are looking at. When $z_oz_i>0$ the coefficient multiplying $x^2+y^2$ is positive and the surface closes onto itself as an ellipsoid, whereas when $z_oz_i<0$ that coefficient turns negative and the surface opens as a hyperboloid. There is also a beautiful limit waiting in \ref{eq:ReflectionConic}, because if we push the object to infinity, $z_o\to-\infty$, the conic degenerates into a paraboloid, which is the case that rules every telescope. We are going to walk through each of these situations, since each one corresponds to a real optical instrument.

        \subsection{The ellipsoidal mirror}
                
                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/EllipsoidalMirror.pdf}
                    \caption{Ellipsoidal mirror. The object $F_1$ and the image $F_2$ coincide with the two foci of the ellipse, so every ray leaving one focus is reflected exactly onto the other.}
                    \label{fig:EllipsoidalMirror}
                \end{figure}

                Lets start with $z_oz_i>0$, so that the object and the image sit on the same side of the vertex and the mirror is an ellipsoid. Dividing \ref{eq:ReflectionConic} by $\left(\frac{z_o+z_i}{2}\right)^2$ the equation takes the canonical shape
                \begin{equation}
                    \frac{\left(z - \frac{z_o+z_i}{2}\right)^2}{\left(\frac{z_o+z_i}{2}\right)^2} + \frac{x^2+y^2}{z_oz_i} = 1,
                \end{equation}
                and if we baptise the two semi-axes as
                \begin{equation}
                    b^2 = \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4}, \qquad a^2 = z_oz_i,
                \end{equation}
                we recognise a prolate ellipsoid centered at $z = b = \frac{z_o+z_i}{2}$, that is
                \begin{equation}
                    \frac{1}{b^2}\left(z-b\right)^2 + \frac{1}{a^2}(x^2+y^2) = 1.
                \end{equation}
                That the ellipsoid comes out prolate, and not oblate, is not an accident, because
                \begin{equation*}
                    b^2 - a^2 = \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4} - z_oz_i = \frac{(z_o-z_i)^2}{4} \geq 0,
                \end{equation*}
                so the semi-axis along the optical axis is always the largest one and the mirror is stretched along $z$. The distance from the center to each focus is then $c = \sqrt{b^2-a^2} = \frac{|z_o-z_i|}{2}$, and the eccentricity becomes
                \begin{equation}
                    \epsilon = \frac{c}{b} = \frac{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}{b} = \sqrt{1-\frac{a^2}{b^2}} = \frac{|z_o-z_i|}{z_o+z_i}<1,
                \end{equation}
                which confirms once more that we are dealing with an ellipse. The really interesting thing is where the foci land, because measured from the vertex they sit at $z = b\pm c$, that is
                \begin{equation}
                    b-c = \frac{z_o+z_i}{2} - \frac{|z_o-z_i|}{2}, \qquad b+c = \frac{z_o+z_i}{2} + \frac{|z_o-z_i|}{2},
                \end{equation}
                and no matter which of the two conjugate points is the larger one, these two numbers are nothing but $z_o$ and $z_i$ themselves. \textbf{The two foci of the ellipsoid are exactly the object point and the image point.}

                This is the whole magic of the ellipsoidal mirror and the reason it is rigorously stigmatic. An ellipse has the geometric property that any ray leaving one focus is reflected straight into the other focus, because the sum of the two focal distances is constant along the whole curve, and a constant optical path is exactly the Fermat's principle at work. Placing a point source at one focus therefore produces a perfect point image at the other one, with no aberration at all. This is the principle behind the elliptical reflectors used to concentrate the light coming from one focus onto a detector sitting at the other, and it is the same property that makes the whispering galleries carry a voice from one focus of the room to the other.

                When both $z_o$ and $z_i$ are negative the algebra is identical once we define $b = -\frac{z_o+z_i}{2}>0$, so that the ellipsoid is now centered at $z=-b$ and its equation reads $\frac{1}{b^2}(z+b)^2 + \frac{1}{a^2}(x^2+y^2)=1$. The eccentricity is the same and the foci again fall on $z_o$ and $z_i$, the only difference being that both conjugate points now live on the opposite side of the vertex.


        \subsection{The hyperboloidal mirror}
                
                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/HyperboloidMirror.pdf}
                    \caption{Hyperboloid mirror. The rays from the object $F_1$ diverge as they were emited by a virtual image locaed in $F_2$.}
                    \label{fig:HypberboloidMirror}
                \end{figure}

                Now we let the product change sign, $z_oz_i<0$, which happens when the object and the image sit on opposite sides of the mirror and one of them is virtual. The coefficient of $x^2+y^2$ in \ref{eq:ReflectionConic} becomes negative, so writing $a^2 = -z_oz_i>0$ while keeping $b^2 = \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4}$ the canonical form is now
                \begin{equation}
                    \frac{1}{b^2}(z-b)^2 - \frac{1}{a^2}(x^2+y^2) = 1,
                \end{equation}
                a hyperboloid of two sheets around the optical axis. The focal distance is measured with a plus sign this time, $c = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, and a short computation gives again $c = \frac{|z_o-z_i|}{2}$, so the eccentricity is
                \begin{equation}
                    \epsilon = \frac{c}{|b|} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}{|b|} = \sqrt{1+\frac{a^2}{b^2}} = \frac{|z_o-z_i|}{|z_o+z_i|}>1,
                \end{equation}
                which is greater than one exactly because $z_oz_i<0$ forces $|z_o-z_i|>|z_o+z_i|$. And just as before, the foci $z = b\pm c$ collapse onto $z_o$ and $z_i$, so once more the two conjugate points are the two foci of the conic.

                The hyperboloidal mirror is therefore stigmatic between its two foci as well, but with a twist, because since the foci lie on opposite sheets one of the conjugate points is always virtual. This is precisely the behavior exploited by the convex secondary mirror of a Cassegrain telescope, where the light that is already converging toward the focus of the primary mirror is intercepted by a hyperboloidal secondary and redirected, without adding any aberration, to a more comfortable focus behind the primary. The stigmatic pair of the hyperboloid is what lets these compact folded telescopes keep a perfect image on the axis.

        \subsection{Special cases: the spherical and the plane mirror}

                Two very familiar mirrors are hiding inside \ref{eq:ReflectionConic} as degenerate cases. The first one appears when the object and the image coincide, $z_o = z_i = R$. The coefficient of $x^2+y^2$ becomes $\frac{(2R)^2}{4R^2}=1$ and the conic reduces to $z^2 - 2Rz + x^2+y^2 = 0$, and completing the square by adding ${\color{blue}R^2}$ to both sides we get
                \begin{equation}
                    (z-R)^2 + x^2+y^2 = R^2,
                \end{equation}
                which is simply a sphere of radius $R$ centered at $z=R$. This tells us that a spherical mirror is rigorously stigmatic for one very special pair of points, both of them sitting together at the center of the sphere, since a source placed at the center sends every ray along a radius, the ray strikes the surface perpendicularly and comes straight back on itself, forming a perfect image right on top of the source. For any other position the sphere is only approximately stigmatic, and this is the origin of the spherical aberration that spoils cheap mirrors and that the conics above were designed to cure.

                The second degenerate case is $z_o = -z_i$, for which the sum $z_o+z_i$ vanishes. Both the linear term and the whole right hand side of \ref{eq:ReflectionConic} die at once, and the equation collapses to $z^2 = 0$, that is, the plane $z=0$. We recover the ordinary plane mirror, which is stigmatic for every point and always produces an image symmetric to the object at $z_i=-z_o$, which is nothing more than the everyday statement that a plane mirror puts your reflection as far behind the glass as you stand in front of it.

        \subsection{The parabolic mirror and objects at infinity}

                \begin{figure}[ht]
                    \centering
                    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter 2/Figures/ParaboloidMirror.pdf}
                    \caption{Parabolic mirror obtained as the limit $z_o\to-\infty$. The rays coming parallel to the axis from an object at infinity are all focused onto the single point $z=z_i$.}
                    \label{fig:ParabolicMirror}
                \end{figure}

                The most important mirror of all comes from letting the object recede to infinity, $z_o\to-\infty$, which is the situation of a telescope pointed at a star. Going back to \ref{eq:ReflectionConic} and expanding the square on the left, the terms ${\color{red}\frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4}}$ appear on both sides and cancel,
                \begin{equation*}
                    z^2 - z(z_o+z_i) + {\color{red}\frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4}} + \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4z_oz_i}(x^2+y^2) = {\color{red}\frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4}},
                \end{equation*}
                leaving the expanded conic
                \begin{equation*}
                    z^2 - z(z_o+z_i) + \frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4z_oz_i}(x^2+y^2) = 0.
                \end{equation*}
                Dividing everything by $z_o$ and taking the limit $z_o\to-\infty$, the term $z^2/z_o$ vanishes, the factor $\frac{z_o+z_i}{z_o}\to 1$ and $\frac{(z_o+z_i)^2}{4z_o^2z_i}\to\frac{1}{4z_i}$, so we are left with the remarkably clean surface
                \begin{equation}\label{eq:ParabolicMirror}
                    x^2+y^2 = 4z_iz,
                \end{equation}
                a paraboloid of revolution whose opening is fixed by the sign of $z_i$.

                The paraboloid is the crown jewel of the reflective optics. A parabola has the defining property that every ray arriving parallel to its axis is reflected exactly through the focus, so a bundle of parallel rays coming from a star, which is an object at infinity, is gathered into a single perfect point at $z=z_i$, the focus of the mirror. There is no chromatic aberration, because the reflection does not depend on the wavelength, and there is no spherical aberration, because the surface is a true paraboloid instead of a sphere. This is why every serious reflecting telescope, from the first design of Newton to the segmented primary of the James Webb, uses a parabolic mirror as its light collecting surface, and it is also why the same shape reappears in the satellite dishes and the searchlights, where the ray tracing simply runs the other way and a source placed at the focus is turned into a parallel beam.
