\section{A historical context of coherence theory}

	At its heart, coherence asks a single, almost childlike question: how much does one
	thing resemble another, and does that resemblance survive as time goes by? It sounds too
	simple to matter, and yet the search for a rigorous answer to that question grew, over
	three centuries, into one of the great unifying ideas of physics: a language that
	explains why some light sources cast crisp interference fringes while others cast none
	at all, why a laser beam behaves so differently from a candle flame, how radio
	astronomers photograph the shadow of a black hole without ever building a telescope the
	size of the Earth, and why the delicate superpositions inside a quantum computer are so
	maddeningly hard to keep alive. The story of coherence is really two stories that grew up
	separately and only later discovered they were talking about the same thing: one begins
	at a gambling table in seventeenth century France, and the other begins in a darkened
	optics laboratory. It is worth following both threads from the start.

	The first thread is the story of correlation, and it begins, fittingly, with a bet.
	In 1654 the nobleman and gambler Antoine Gombaud, better known as the Chevalier de
	Méré, wrote to Blaise Pascal asking how the stakes of an interrupted game of dice should
	fairly be divided between two players. The resulting correspondence between Pascal and
	Pierre de Fermat, working out the mathematics of expectation and combinatorics needed to
	answer such questions, is generally credited as the birth of probability theory. Their
	ideas matured slowly. In 1713, sixty years later, Jacob Bernoulli's posthumous book
	\textit{Ars Conjectandi} \cite{bernoulli1713} proved the law of large numbers, the
	remarkable fact that if you repeat a random experiment often enough, the observed
	frequency of an outcome settles down, ever more reliably, around its true theoretical
	probability. A century later still, in 1812, Pierre Simon Laplace gathered these
	scattered results, together with his own theory of how new evidence should update our
	beliefs (what we now call Bayesian inference), into the monumental
	\textit{Théorie Analytique des Probabilités} \cite{laplace1812theorie}. What was still
	missing from this whole tradition was a number, a single figure that could say not just
	how uncertain one quantity is, but how strongly two different quantities move together.

	That missing number arrived through an unexpected route: the very practical business
	of dealing with noisy measurements. In 1809, working out how to fit the imperfect,
	scatter riddled observations of planetary orbits, Carl Friedrich Gauss introduced the
	method of least squares in his \textit{Theoria Motus} \cite{gauss1809theoria}, a
	technique built entirely on the idea that errors cluster statistically around the truth.
	Decades later, in the 1880s, the polymath Francis Galton, fascinated by how strongly a
	child's height tends to track its parents', coined the very word ``correlation'' to
	describe exactly this kind of joint tendency \cite{galton1888correlations}. It fell to
	his student Karl Pearson, in 1896, to turn Galton's intuition into a precise formula: the
	Pearson correlation coefficient \cite{pearson1896regression}, a single number, always
	between minus one and plus one, that tells you at a glance whether two quantities march
	together, drift apart, or ignore each other completely. For the first time, resemblance
	between two things could be measured rather than merely felt.

	The second thread, meanwhile, was unfolding almost entirely without reference to the
	first, in the study of light itself. In 1801, Thomas Young let sunlight fall through two
	narrow slits and watched it paint a row of bright and dark bands on a screen behind
	them \cite{young1804bakerian}, the unmistakable signature of two waves sometimes
	reinforcing and sometimes cancelling one another. For those fringes to appear at all, the
	light leaving the two slits had to keep a steady, dependable phase relationship with
	itself, a property nobody yet had a name for, though everybody could see its footprint on
	the screen. Between 1815 and 1820, Augustin Jean Fresnel built the full mathematical
	theory of light as a transverse wave, and with it interference and polarisation stopped
	being curiosities and became consequences of a single physical picture. And yet a puzzle
	quietly remained. Young's beam, freshly split from a single narrow slit, produced fringes
	beautifully. But ordinary daylight, or the light of a candle, or of the Sun itself, never
	produced anything of the sort when simply allowed to interfere with a delayed copy of
	itself beyond a certain separation. Something about real, everyday light was different
	from the idealised, perfectly regular wave that Fresnel's mathematics described, and for
	a hundred years nobody could quite say what.

	The answer, when it finally came in the twentieth century, is that ordinary light
	sources are not single, orderly waves at all. A hot filament, a flame, or the surface of
	the Sun is an enormous crowd of independent atoms, each one emitting its own short burst
	of light and then falling silent, entirely out of step with its neighbours, so that the
	overall wave forms and reforms its phase billions of times each second in a way that is,
	for all practical purposes, random. Describing such light properly meant marrying the two
	separate traditions that had grown up in isolation: the statistical machinery of Pascal,
	Gauss, Galton and Pearson had to be grafted onto the wave optics of Young and Fresnel.
	That graft is exactly what the word ``coherence'' now names: not whether a wave has a
	phase, every wave does, but whether that phase remains predictable enough, for long
	enough and over a wide enough region, to leave a visible trace when the wave is made to
	interfere with a copy of itself.

	The decisive mathematical step was taken in the 1930s. In 1934 the Dutch physicist
	Pieter Hendrik van Cittert worked out how the statistical correlations in the light from
	an incoherent, extended source, a distant star, for instance, change as that light
	travels away from the source \cite{vancittert1934}, and in 1938 Frits Zernike
	sharpened and generalised the result into the theorem that today carries both their
	names \cite{zernike1938}. The van Cittert Zernike theorem makes a beautifully
	counterintuitive claim: light that starts out utterly incoherent, with every point on the
	source flickering independently of every other, becomes measurably, predictably more
	coherent purely by virtue of travelling a long distance. This is not a minor footnote. It
	is the physical principle that allows astronomers to measure the angular size of stars
	far too small for any telescope to resolve directly, by comparing the light collected at
	two separated points and watching how the fringes fade as the separation grows, exactly
	the trick Albert Michelson and Francis Pease used in 1920 to measure the diameter of the
	red giant Betelgeuse \cite{michelson1921diameter}. Decades later, the very same theorem,
	scaled up from a handful of metres to a network of radio dishes spanning the whole planet,
	is what let the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration reconstruct, in 2019, the first
	direct image of a black hole.

	Classical coherence theory found its definitive, mature form in the work of Max Born and
	Emil Wolf, whose treatise \textit{Principles of Optics} \cite{born1999principles} gathered
	every strand into a single object, the mutual coherence function, which simply measures
	how correlated the light wave is between two points in space and two moments in time.
	Squeezed inside that one function are both of the qualities an experimenter actually
	cares about: the temporal coherence, essentially how pure a colour the light is and for
	how long it keeps a steady rhythm with itself, and the spatial coherence, how faithfully
	the wave stays in step across an extended region rather than just at a single point.
	Leonard Mandel and Wolf later expanded this picture into the full treatise
	\textit{Optical Coherence and Quantum Optics} \cite{mandel1995optical}, sharpening the
	practical notions of coherence time and coherence length that every laboratory now takes
	for granted. None of this stayed confined to the blackboard. The Michelson interferometer
	of the 1890s already exploited temporal coherence to measure wavelengths with a precision
	no ruler could match, and the invention of the laser in 1960, whose light is coherent to
	a degree no natural source ever achieves, turned coherence from a subject of theoretical
	fascination into the working principle behind fibre optic communication, precision
	metrology, holography and modern medicine.

	Nor does coherence belong to optics alone. Wherever many independent parts of a system
	can be made to oscillate in step, something with exactly the same mathematical anatomy
	appears. In condensed matter physics, superconductivity is, at bottom, a coherence
	phenomenon: below a critical temperature, an enormous population of paired electrons
	locks into a single, shared quantum phase across an entire macroscopic sample, which is
	why a supercurrent flows without any resistance at all. Nuclear magnetic resonance and
	the magnetic resonance imaging built upon it work by tipping billions of nuclear spins
	into a common, coherent precession and then watching, with exquisite sensitivity, how
	quickly that shared rhythm decays. Every atomic clock, and with it the entire Global
	Positioning System, depends on an oscillator whose phase stays coherent for as long as
	humanly achievable. Coherence, in short, is not a peculiarity of light; it is what allows
	countless independent actors, electrons, spins, atoms or photons, to behave, briefly and
	fragilely, as one.

	While all of this classical machinery was being consolidated, an entirely separate and
	stranger story was unfolding in the quantum world. Its opening scene took place in 1956,
	when Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Twiss, trying to measure the angular sizes of
	stars with radio and then optical detectors, found that photons arriving from a thermal
	source at two separated detectors were not independent: they tended to arrive in
	correlated little bunches \cite{hanburybrown1956correlation}. The result seemed to many
	physicists at the time to be flatly impossible. Photons, everyone assumed, should behave
	like classical, independent particles once detected, and no such correlation should
	survive the act of measurement; a real and lively controversy followed before the effect
	was accepted. Untangling it properly required a fully quantum theory of coherence, and
	that theory was supplied in 1963, almost simultaneously and independently, by Roy Glauber
	\cite{glauber1963quantum} and George Sudarshan \cite{sudarshan1963equivalence}. Glauber
	introduced the coherent states, the quantum states of light that behave as closely as
	quantum mechanics permits to Fresnel's classical waves, and defined a whole hierarchy of
	quantum correlation functions capable of explaining Hanbury Brown and Twiss's bunching
	rigorously, and of predicting its mirror image, photon antibunching, a behaviour so
	stubbornly non classical that no wave theory of light could ever reproduce it, later
	confirmed in the laboratory. Sudarshan, working from a different direction, found the
	precise mathematical boundary, now called the Glauber Sudarshan representation, that
	separates light whose quantum nature can safely be ignored from light for which it
	cannot. For this quantum theory of optical coherence, Glauber received the Nobel Prize in
	2005, and the field he founded is what we now call quantum optics.

	Quantum coherence turned out to matter for reasons that reach far beyond photon
	counting. It is quantum coherence, the delicate, phase locked superposition of many
	possible states at once, that makes a quantum bit fundamentally different from an
	ordinary one, and it is the loss of that coherence through unavoidable contact with the
	environment, a process physicists call decoherence, that explains why the everyday world
	around us looks so stubbornly classical even though it is built from quantum parts.
	Keeping coherence alive for long enough to be useful is, to this day, the single greatest
	engineering obstacle standing between today's quantum computers and tomorrow's. The same
	idea reappears in the coldest matter physicists know how to make: in 1995 Eric Cornell,
	Carl Wieman and Wolfgang Ketterle cooled clouds of atoms to nanokelvin temperatures and
	watched millions of them collapse into a single, shared quantum state, a Bose Einstein
	condensate, in effect one enormous coherent matter wave, an achievement recognised with
	the Nobel Prize in 2001. It even reaches into biology: careful experiments on the
	light harvesting complexes that plants and certain bacteria use to capture sunlight have
	found that quantum coherence in the transfer of that energy survives, astonishingly, for
	measurable fractions of a second at room temperature, hinting that evolution may have
	stumbled upon and exploited quantum coherence long before physicists gave it a name.

	Binding the statistical and the wave pictures of coherence together, classical and
	quantum alike, is a single piece of mathematics: the Wiener Khinchin theorem, developed
	independently by Norbert Wiener in 1930 \cite{wiener1930generalized} and Aleksandr
	Khinchin in 1934 \cite{khinchin1934korrelationstheorie}, which states that the
	correlation of a fluctuating signal with itself and the way that signal's energy is
	spread across frequency are simply two faces of the same object, translated into one
	another by a Fourier transform. It is, as later chapters will show, the quiet
	mathematical hinge on which the whole subject turns.

	In the twenty first century coherence has acquired one further identity: it is now
	understood, and formally quantified, as a genuine physical resource, something that can
	be created, spent and depleted much like energy or entanglement, with its own rigorous
	accounting rules inside the broader theory of quantum information
	\cite{baumgratz2014quantifying}. Seen end to end, the history of coherence runs from a
	seventeenth century argument about dice, through the statistics of inherited height and
	the interference of sunlight, to the imaging of a black hole and the fragile inner
	workings of a quantum processor. In its classical guise it explains why stars twinkle
	the way they do, why a laser can cut steel while a torch cannot, and why an interferometer
	the size of a planet can photograph something no single telescope ever could. In its
	quantum guise it explains why the world of atoms feels so unlike the world of tables and
	chairs, and it now sits at the very centre of the effort to build technologies that think
	and communicate in ways no classical machine ever could. Coherence, whether counted in
	correlated dice throws, interfering light waves or entangled qubits, is ultimately always
	the same thing: order, briefly and remarkably, surviving in a world that constantly
	conspires to erase it.

\section{How is statistical similarity quantified?}

	Suppose we have a completely random phenomenon, such as the electromagnetic radiation
	emitted by an incandescent bulb that is not in thermodynamic equilibrium, the number of
	cars in some capital city throughout the day, the motion of an ion in a plasma, or
	something as simple as throwing a ball into the air under non-ideal conditions.

	When we wish to analyse statistically a single object or random variable, the familiar
	tools appear: the probability density, the mean value, the standard deviation, the
	kurtosis, and so on. What happens when we want to study at least two random variables and
	how they relate? When we wish to study the statistics of two or more random variables we
	must study the statistical similarity between them; but statistical similarity is a rather
	broad concept, since we may have similarity in the mean values, or in the standard
	deviations, or in the probability densities. To address this problem we return to linear
	algebra.

	If we have two vectors $\vec{A},\vec{B} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the dot product
	$\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$ is interpreted as the magnitude of the projection of $\vec{A}$ onto
	$\vec{B}$ and vice versa; it can also be read as \textit{how much of $\vec{A}$ lies along
	$\vec{B}$}.

	It is worth pausing on what that operation actually does mechanically, because the whole
	of coherence theory is built on this single gesture. Written out component by component,
	the dot product is
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:DotComponents}
		\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i B_i,
	\end{equation}
	that is, \textit{multiply the two lists entry by entry and add up the results}. Nothing
	more. And yet the sign of each individual term already carries exactly the information we
	are after: whenever $A_i$ and $B_i$ lean the same way, their product is positive and pushes
	the sum upwards; whenever they lean opposite ways, the product is negative and drags it
	down. If the two vectors tend to agree along most of their components, the positive terms
	dominate and the total comes out large; if they systematically disagree, the total comes
	out large but negative; and if they agree just as often as they disagree, the terms cancel
	one another in the sum and the total collapses to nearly zero. So ``multiply the pairs and
	add'' is already, all by itself, a similarity meter, and everything that follows in this
	chapter is that same gesture applied to progressively richer objects.

	This behaviour can be extrapolated to continuous vector spaces, where one
	usually employs the notion of inner product. The step is smaller than it looks: a function
	is simply a vector with a continuum of components, since instead of the finite list
	$A_1,A_2,\dots,A_n$ we now carry the values $f_A(x)$, one for every $x$. The index $i$
	becomes the label $x$, the sum over a discrete index becomes an integral over a continuous
	one, and the dot product becomes the inner product. If $f_A , f_B$ are two elements of the
	Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ of square integrable functions of one real variable, one
	can define an inner product as

	\begin{equation}\label{eq:ProdIn}
		\braket{f_A,f_B} = \int_\mathbb{R} f_A^*(x)f_B(x) W(x)\,dx,
	\end{equation}

	where the complex conjugate on the first factor is what keeps
	$\braket{f_A,f_A}$ real and non negative even when the functions are complex, as the
	optical fields of this chapter will be, and where $W(x)$ is some weight
	function.\footnote{$W(x)$ receives this name because, as it
	says, it gives a weight to each value the integrand may take; there are no uniform
	contributions, but rather each possible value of the integrand within the domain
	contributes weighted by the value of $W$ at that point.} The interpretation is still
	valid: this can be read as how much the function $f_A$ over all the reals coincides with
	the function $f_B$ over all the reals.

	To reach the notion of correlation from linear algebra we use a bit of braket notation,
	where the ket $\ket{f_A}$ represents a vector of an arbitrary Hilbert space $L^2$ and the
	bra $\bra{f_A}$ represents an element of the dual of the same Hilbert space,\footnote{The
	Hilbert space is a particular case of the $L^p$ spaces, the space of $p$-norm
	Lebesgue-integrable functionals; for Hilbert $p=2$, and it has the property that the dual
	of $L^2$ is also $L^2$, which lets quantum mechanics arise naturally in this space.} and
	the inner product of a state with itself, $\braket{f_A,f_A}= \braket{f_A|f_A}$, accumulates
	the probability of finding the system anywhere at all; it is the integrand $|f_A(q)|^2$,
	rather than the completed integral, that plays the role of the probability density of
	finding $f_A$ in a state $q \pm \triangle q$, where $q$ represents some
	generalised coordinate. Suppose that $f_A,f_B \in L^2$ have the form

	\begin{equation}
		\ket{f_A} = \hat{A}\ket{\Psi_A}, \quad \ket{f_B} = \hat{B}\ket{\Psi_B},
	\end{equation}

	where $\hat{A},\hat{B}$ are the operators of the physical quantities of
	$\ket{\Psi_A},\ket{\Psi_B}$, so that we may interpret $\ket{f_A},\ket{f_B}$ as the wave
	functions steered by their expected value. Using the inner product \eqref{eq:ProdIn} with
	unit weight function, $W=1$, the inner product reads

	\begin{equation}
		\braket{f_A | f_B} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \hat{A}\hat{B} \braket{\Psi_A|\Psi_B}\, d^nq = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \hat{A}\hat{B}\, P(A;B)\, d^nq,
	\end{equation}

	where we see that the similarity between $\ket{f_A}$ and $\ket{f_B}$ can be viewed as the
	product of $\hat{A}$ with $\hat{B}$ times the probability density of having states $A$ and
	$B$.\footnote{This last step is deliberately heuristic: we are treating the operators as
	though they could simply be pulled out of the inner product and replaced by the values they
	measure, which is only legitimate when acting on their own eigenstates. The aim here is not
	a derivation but a bridge, to make plausible where the definition that follows comes from;
	the rigorous treatment belongs to \cite{mandel1995optical}.} That is, the similarity, or how
	\textit{correlated} two variables are, can be seen as
	the integral of the product of the variables times their joint probability density.

	With this analogy in mind we can understand correlation as a first measure of the
	similarity between one or more variables across space and time. If $x_1(\vec{r},t_1),
	x_2(\vec{r}',t_2)$ are two random variables and $p_2(x_1,t_1;x_2,t_2)$ is the joint
	probability density, then the correlation between $x_1,x_2$ is defined as

	\begin{equation}\label{eq:CorrFunc}
		\Gamma(\vec{r},\vec{r}';t_1,t_2) = E\left\{ x_1(\vec{r},t_1) x_2^*(\vec{r}',t_2)\right\}
		= \int\!\!\int x_1 x_2^* \; p_2(x_1,t_1;x_2,t_2)\, dx_1 dx_2,
	\end{equation}

	that is, as the ensemble average of $x_1\cdot x_2^*$, weighted by how likely each pair of
	values is to occur together. The conjugation is placed on the second factor here to agree
	with the convention used for the coherence function in the next section; for real valued
	variables it does nothing at all, and for complex ones it merely conjugates $\Gamma$ as a
	whole, leaving every magnitude we shall care about untouched.

	Structurally this is the same object we have been building all along. The sum over
	components in \eqref{eq:DotComponents} has simply become an average over the ensemble, and
	the role of the weight function $W$ in \eqref{eq:ProdIn} is now played by the joint
	probability density $p_2$, which is the natural weight to use when the ``components'' being
	paired up are the possible outcomes of a random experiment rather than the entries of a
	list. Correlation, in other words, is the dot product of two random variables, taken in the
	only sense available when the quantities involved refuse to hold still.

	The same sign argument that made the dot product a similarity meter now tells us how to read
	$\Gamma$, and it is worth spelling out the three cases explicitly. If the two variables rise
	and fall together, so that they are large at the same instants and small at the same
	instants, then the product $x_1 x_2^*$ is predominantly positive, every realisation of the
	experiment contributes with the same sign, and the average survives as a large positive
	number. If they move in strict opposition, one peaking whenever the other troughs, the
	product is predominantly negative and the average survives as a large negative number, which
	is just as informative: perfect anticorrelation is a form of perfect similarity, merely with
	a sign attached. But if the two variables know nothing about each other, then for every
	realisation in which the product happens to come out positive there is, somewhere in the
	ensemble, an equally likely realisation in which it comes out negative, the contributions
	annihilate one another, and the average collapses to zero. The averaging is
	therefore not an incidental technicality but the entire mechanism: it is precisely what
	destroys the terms that carry no shared information while allowing the terms that do to
	accumulate.

	Two features of the definition \eqref{eq:CorrFunc} deserve a comment, because both differ
	from the Pearson coefficient met in the historical introduction. The first is that we have
	not subtracted the mean values before multiplying. Subtracting them would produce the
	covariance, which asks how the \textit{fluctuations} about the average conspire; keeping
	them, as we do here, asks about the total quantity, and for the optical fields of this
	chapter the distinction happily evaporates, since a thermal field oscillates symmetrically
	about zero and its mean vanishes on its own. The second is that we have not divided by
	anything, so $\Gamma$ still carries the physical units of the fields it was built from and
	its size depends on how intense the light happens to be, not only on how similar it is. A
	correlation of $10^{-6}$ between two feeble beams may represent far tighter agreement than a
	correlation of unity between two powerful ones. Repairing this, by dividing $\Gamma$ by its
	own value at zero separation, which is precisely the intensity, so that the result is a pure
	number confined between zero and one that measures similarity and similarity alone, is
	exactly the step that turns the correlation function into the \textit{complex degree of
	coherence}, and it is the point the next section works towards.

\section{Random variables}\label{sec:RandomVariables}

	In the previous section we spoke freely of random variables, of joint probability
	densities and of ensemble averages, and we did so on purpose, because the aim there was to
	make the definition of the correlation function plausible rather than to justify it. That
	debt must now be paid. Coherence theory is, from beginning to end, a theory \textit{about}
	averages, and an average that is not defined precisely defines nothing precisely. Worse,
	the objects we shall manipulate are not averages of numbers but averages over an infinite
	collection of entire field histories, and for such objects naive intuition is not merely
	imprecise, it is occasionally wrong.

	The purpose of this section is therefore to build the notion of a random variable from the
	ground up, in the measure theoretic language in which it was cast by Kolmogorov
	\citep{kolmogorov1933grundbegriffe}, and then to apply it to the vector electromagnetic
	field, constructing explicitly the probability space on which the whole of the following
	theory lives. Every ingredient will be given its physical reading as it appears, because
	each one of them corresponds to something an optician does in a laboratory.

	\subsection{The sample space and the physical meaning of an event}

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Sample space)} A \textbf{sample space} is a nonempty set $\Omega$. Its
			elements $\omega\in\Omega$ are called \emph{elementary outcomes} or
			\emph{sample points}.
		\end{myDef}

		The temptation is to read $\omega$ as a number, and it is essential to resist it. In
		optics an elementary outcome is not a field value and not a measurement; it is one
		complete \emph{realisation} of the source, that is, one entire spacetime history that
		the field could have had. A thermal lamp contains of the order of $10^{20}$ atoms
		radiating independently, each with an emission instant and an initial phase that we
		neither control nor know. One particular assignment of all those microscopic details,
		together with everything that follows from it by Maxwell's equations, is one $\omega$.
		The set of all such assignments is $\Omega$.

		This is precisely the picture that Figure~\ref{fig:randomvar} will show in the next
		section, where several curves $^{(i)}E(\vec{r},t)$ are drawn one above the other: each
		curve is one $\omega$, and the label $i$ is only a bookkeeping device, since in general
		$\Omega$ is uncountable. What the laboratory delivers on any given afternoon is a single
		$\omega$, chosen by nature and not revealed to us. The whole of statistical optics
		consists in extracting statements about $\Omega$ from access to one of its points.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Event)} An \textbf{event} is a subset $A\subseteq\Omega$.
		\end{myDef}

		The physical content of this definition is that an event is a statement about
		the realisation that can be declared true or false. The statement ``the field at
		$\vec{r}_{0}$ at the instant $t_{0}$ exceeded the value $E_{0}$'' is not a number; it is
		the set of all realisations for which that happens to be the case. To say that the event
		occurred is to say that the $\omega$ nature selected belongs to that subset. Events and
		statements are the same thing seen from two sides, and this identification is what
		allows probability to be built out of set theory.

	\subsection{The sigma algebra, or which questions may be asked}

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Sigma algebra)} A \textbf{sigma algebra} on $\Omega$ is a collection
			$\mathcal{F}$ of subsets of $\Omega$ such that
			\begin{enumerate}
				\item $\Omega\in\mathcal{F}$,
				\item if $A\in\mathcal{F}$ then $A^{c} = \Omega\setminus A\in\mathcal{F}$,
				\item if $A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\dots\in\mathcal{F}$ then
				$\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in\mathcal{F}$.
			\end{enumerate}
			The pair $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ is called a \emph{measurable space}.
		\end{myDef}

		A sigma algebra looks like a piece of bookkeeping and is in fact a physical statement.
		It is the \emph{catalogue of admissible questions}, the list of statements to which
		the theory is prepared to attach a probability, and the three axioms are exactly the
		three properties that any honest catalogue of questions must have.

		The first says that the question ``did anything at all happen?'' is admissible. The
		second says that if we can ask whether an event occurred, we can ask whether it did not,
		which is the formal expression of the fact that an apparatus capable of detecting
		something is by that very fact capable of detecting its absence. The third says that if
		each of a sequence of questions is admissible, so is the question of whether at least
		one of them holds.

		The word \textit{countable} in the third axiom is not a technical convenience but a
		genuine physical restriction, and it deserves to be pointed out. Any real experimental
		protocol is a \textit{sequence} of operations: one records a reading, then another, then
		another. No laboratory has ever performed an uncountable number of operations, and the
		theory is built so as to promise nothing about what would happen if one could.

		One might ask why we do not simply take $\mathcal{F}$ to be the collection of
		\textit{all} subsets of $\Omega$ and be done with it. The answer is that we are not
		allowed to. Vitali showed in 1905 \citep{vitali1905problema} that on the real line there
		exist sets so pathological that no translation invariant notion of size can be assigned
		to them without contradiction. If we insisted on measuring everything we would be forced
		into inconsistency, and the sigma algebra is precisely the device that keeps such
		monsters outside the theory while retaining every set that a physicist would ever have
		occasion to ask about.

		The example we shall need constantly is the following.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Borel sigma algebra)} The \emph{Borel sigma algebra}
			$\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ is the smallest sigma algebra on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
			containing all the open sets.
		\end{myDef}

		Physically, $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is generated by the questions ``does the reading
		lie between $a$ and $b$?'', together with everything that can be built from countably
		many such questions by negation and union. This is exactly the class of questions that a
		real detector, which has a finite resolution and reports an interval rather than a
		point, is able to answer. The Borel sigma algebra is not an abstraction imposed on
		physics; it is the mathematical transcription of what a graduated instrument does.

	\subsection{Measure, the Lebesgue measure and the probability measure}

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Measure)} A \textbf{measure} on a measurable space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$
			is a function $\mu:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty]$ such that $\mu(\emptyset)=0$ and such
			that, for every sequence $A_{1},A_{2},\dots$ of pairwise disjoint elements of
			$\mathcal{F}$,
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:CountableAdditivity}
				\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n}).
			\end{equation}
			The triple $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)$ is a \emph{measure space}.
		\end{myDef}

		A measure is a consistent way of assigning a \textit{size}. Condition
		\eqref{eq:CountableAdditivity} is countable additivity, and it states nothing more
		exotic than that the size of a whole assembled out of separate pieces is the sum of the
		sizes of the pieces. That is what the word size means operationally, and a function
		which violated it would not deserve the name.

		Among all measures one is singled out by the geometry of space itself.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Lebesgue measure)} The \emph{Lebesgue measure} $\lambda$ is the unique
			translation invariant measure on $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ satisfying
			$\lambda\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}[a_{j},b_{j}]\right) = \prod_{j=1}^{n}(b_{j}-a_{j})$.
		\end{myDef}

		The Lebesgue measure \citep{lebesgue1902integrale} is the mathematical form of the words
		\textit{length}, \textit{area}, \textit{volume} and \textit{duration}. Every integral we
		have written so far in these notes has been an integral against $\lambda$: when we
		integrated over an aperture in the theory of diffraction we were measuring \textit{how
		much aperture}, and when we integrate over the response time of a detector we are
		measuring \textit{how long we looked}. The Lebesgue measure knows about the extent of
		the domain and it knows nothing whatever about probability.

		It is worth insisting on this separation, because in coherence theory both measures
		appear at once and they do entirely different jobs. The Lebesgue measure records
		how long the detector integrated and how large the aperture was; the probability measure
		records how likely each realisation of the source was. The time average of a single
		beam is an integral against $\lambda$ on the time axis; the ensemble average over
		realisations is an integral against $P$ on $\Omega$. These are different operations on
		different spaces, and the fact that they so often agree is not a triviality but a
		theorem with hypotheses, to which we return at the end of this section.

		The Lebesgue measure also supplies the missing justification for the densities of the
		previous section. When in \eqref{eq:CorrFunc} we wrote
		$p_{2}(x_{1},t_{1};x_{2},t_{2})\,dx_{1}dx_{2}$ we were tacitly assuming that the
		probability measure on the space of values is absolutely continuous with respect to
		$\lambda$, so that by the theorem of Radon and Nikodym
		\citep{nikodym1930generalisation} it possesses a density. That is a physical hypothesis
		and not a formality: it asserts that no field value occurs with strictly positive
		probability, that is, that the source has no infinitely sharp features. A perfectly
		monochromatic and perfectly stabilised laser would violate it, since its distribution
		would be concentrated on a circle in the complex plane, a set of zero Lebesgue measure,
		and no density with respect to the plane would exist.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Probability space)} A \textbf{probability measure} is a measure $P$ with
			$P(\Omega)=1$. The triple $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ is called a \emph{probability
			space}.
		\end{myDef}

		The normalisation $P(\Omega)=1$ is the statement that the source does \textit{something}:
		some realisation occurs, and the alternatives listed in $\Omega$ are exhaustive.
		Countable additivity, read now in $P$, is the statement that mutually exclusive
		alternatives have probabilities that add.

		Here lies the crucial physical remark of this subsection. The probability
		measure is where the physics of the source resides. A thermal lamp, a laser and a
		single photon emitter may perfectly well share the same $\Omega$ and the same
		$\mathcal{F}$, since all three could in principle produce any of the same field
		histories; what distinguishes them is that they weight those histories differently. To
		specify a source is to specify $P$, and every statement that coherence theory will make
		about light is, in the last analysis, a statement about $P$.

	\subsection{Random variables and the meaning of measurability}

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Random variable)} Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ be a probability space and
			$(S,\mathcal{S})$ a measurable space. A map $X:\Omega\to S$ is a
			\textbf{random variable} if it is \emph{measurable}, that is, if
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:Measurability}
				X^{-1}(B) = \left\{\omega\in\Omega : X(\omega)\in B\right\}\in\mathcal{F}
				\qquad\text{for every } B\in\mathcal{S}.
			\end{equation}
		\end{myDef}

		Two things must be said about this definition, and both are usually said too quickly.

		The first is that \textbf{a random variable is not a variable that is random}. It is a
		perfectly deterministic function. Once the realisation $\omega$ has been fixed,
		$X(\omega)$ is a definite element of $S$, with nothing capricious about it. All the
		randomness lives in the selection of $\omega$, which is to say in our ignorance of which
		realisation nature has produced. The field at a point of spacetime is not fluctuating
		because it is undecided; it is definite in each realisation and we simply do not know
		which realisation we are in.

		The second is that the measurability condition \eqref{eq:Measurability} is the
		mathematical statement that the instrument is a legitimate instrument. It
		demands that the question ``did the reading fall inside $B$?'' be one of the admissible
		questions of $\mathcal{F}$. A non measurable map would be an apparatus that reports an
		answer to a question the theory refuses to assign a probability to, which is a polite
		way of saying that it reports numbers that mean nothing. Measurability is the licence to
		exist.

		Since the outcome $\omega$ is inaccessible, what an experiment actually reconstructs is
		not $P$ but its image.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Law of a random variable)} The \emph{law} or \emph{distribution} of $X$
			is the probability measure $P_{X}$ on $(S,\mathcal{S})$ defined by the push forward
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:PushForward}
				P_{X}(B) = P\left(X^{-1}(B)\right), \qquad B\in\mathcal{S}.
			\end{equation}
			If $P_{X}$ is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, its
			Radon-Nikodym derivative $p = \mathrm{d}P_{X}/\mathrm{d}\lambda$ is the
			\emph{probability density} of $X$.
		\end{myDef}

		The law is what a histogram estimates. Two sources built on completely different sample
		spaces may possess identical laws for a given observable, and are then indistinguishable
		by that observable no matter how long one measures. The density $p$ appearing here is
		exactly the $p_{2}$ that was used without apology in \eqref{eq:CorrFunc}.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Expectation)} The \emph{expectation} of an integrable random variable
			$X$ is the Lebesgue integral of $X$ over the probability space,
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:Expectation}
				E\{X\} = \int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)
				= \int_{S}x\,\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x) = \int_{S}x\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(x),
			\end{equation}
			the second equality being the change of variables induced by \eqref{eq:PushForward}.
		\end{myDef}

		Equation \eqref{eq:Expectation} retroactively legitimises the definition
		\eqref{eq:CorrFunc} of the correlation function. What was written there as an ensemble
		average and what was written as an integral against a joint density are not two
		definitions that happen to agree; they are one definition, given on $\Omega$, and its
		image on the space of values under the push forward. The first form is conceptually
		primary and the second is the one we can compute with.

		There is now a payoff which is worth stating explicitly, because it converts the central
		analogy of the previous section from a metaphor into a theorem. Consider the set of
		random variables of finite second moment,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:L2Omega}
			L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P) = \left\{X : \Omega\to\mathbb{C} \;\middle|\; E\left\{|X|^{2}\right\}<\infty\right\},
		\end{equation}
		equipped with the inner product

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:L2InnerProduct}
			\braket{X,Y} = E\left\{XY^{*}\right\}.
		\end{equation}

		This is a Hilbert space, and comparing \eqref{eq:L2InnerProduct} with the definition
		\eqref{eq:CorrFunc} of the correlation function we see that the correlation
		function is an inner product, not by analogy but literally, in the space of random
		variables of finite energy. The dot product with which the previous section began was
		not an illustrative parallel; it was the same object, waiting for the space to be named.
		The weight function $W$ of \eqref{eq:ProdIn} has become the measure $P$, and the sum
		over components of \eqref{eq:DotComponents} has become the integral over $\Omega$.

		From this identification one inequality follows immediately, and it is the one that
		makes the normalised degree of coherence meaningful. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in
		$L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ reads

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CauchySchwarzProb}
			\left|E\left\{XY^{*}\right\}\right|^{2} \leq E\left\{|X|^{2}\right\}\,E\left\{|Y|^{2}\right\},
		\end{equation}
		so that the correlation of two random variables can never exceed the geometric mean of
		their intensities. When we divide by that geometric mean, as anticipated at the end of
		the previous section, the result is a pure number lying between zero and one. It is,
		quite literally, the cosine of the angle between two vectors in a Hilbert space, and
		this is why a degree of coherence larger than unity is not merely unobserved but
		impossible.

	\subsection{The throw of a die}

		Before the machinery is applied to the electromagnetic field it is worth watching it work
		on the simplest random experiment there is, because every definition of this section has
		got a perfectly concrete meaning there, and the reader who has seen it once on a die
		will recognise it later on a light beam. Consider then a single throw of a fair die. The
		elementary outcomes are the six faces, so the sample space is the finite set
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:DieSampleSpace}
			\Omega = \left\{1,2,3,4,5,6\right\}.
		\end{equation}
		Since $\Omega$ is finite there are no Vitali monsters to keep out, and we may take as
		catalogue of questions the collection of \textit{all} its subsets, the power set
		$\mathcal{F} = 2^{\Omega}$, which contains $2^{6}=64$ events. The statement ``the result
		is even'' is the event $A = \{2,4,6\}$, the statement ``the result is larger than four''
		is $B = \{5,6\}$, and the statement ``the result is even and larger than four'' is their
		intersection $A\cap B = \{6\}$, which is again in $\mathcal{F}$ because, by De Morgan's
		laws, $A\cap B = \left(A^{c}\cup B^{c}\right)^{c}$ is built from the axioms by two
		complements and one union.

		The fairness of the die is a physical statement about the cube, its homogeneity and its
		symmetry, and it is translated into mathematics by the choice of the measure. We demand
		that the six faces be equally likely, $P(\{k\}) = p$ for $k=1,\dots,6$. Since the six
		singletons are pairwise disjoint and their union is $\Omega$, countable additivity
		\eqref{eq:CountableAdditivity} together with the normalisation gives
		\begin{equation*}
			1 = P(\Omega) = P\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{6}\{k\}\right) = \sum_{k=1}^{6}P(\{k\}) = 6p
			\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p = \frac{1}{6},
		\end{equation*}
		and then, for an arbitrary event $A$, which is the disjoint union of its own points,
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:DieMeasure}
			P(A) = \sum_{k\in A}P(\{k\}) = \frac{|A|}{6},
		\end{equation}
		where $|A|$ is the number of elements of $A$. This is the counting measure divided by
		six, and the classical rule ``favourable cases over possible cases'' is nothing but
		\eqref{eq:DieMeasure}. In particular $P(A) = 3/6 = 1/2$ and $P(A\cap B) = 1/6$. A loaded
		die would have exactly the same $\Omega$ and the same $\mathcal{F}$ and a different $P$,
		which is the remark made above about the thermal lamp and the laser, now in miniature.

		The number printed on the upper face is a random variable $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$,
		$X(\omega) = \omega$, and with the power set every map is measurable, so there is
		nothing to check. The interesting situation appears when the observer is \textit{not}
		allowed to ask every question. Suppose that the die is thrown behind a screen and that
		an assistant only tells us whether the result was even or odd. The questions we can
		answer then form the much smaller collection
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:DieParity}
			\mathcal{F}_{\text{par}} = \left\{\emptyset,\ \{2,4,6\},\ \{1,3,5\},\ \Omega\right\},
		\end{equation}
		which is easily checked to be a sigma algebra: it contains $\Omega$, the complement of
		each of its members is again a member, and any union of its members is one of the four.
		With respect to $\mathcal{F}_{\text{par}}$ the face value is no longer a random variable,
		because
		\begin{equation*}
			X^{-1}\left(\{6\}\right) = \left\{\omega\in\Omega : X(\omega) = 6\right\} = \{6\}
			\notin\mathcal{F}_{\text{par}},
		\end{equation*}
		so that the condition \eqref{eq:Measurability} fails for $B=\{6\}$. In words, a
		device that announces ``six'' cannot be built out of an assistant who only knows the
		parity; it would answer a question that the available information does not allow to be
		asked. The parity itself, $Y(\omega) = 1$ if $\omega$ is even and $Y(\omega)=0$
		otherwise, is perfectly measurable with respect to $\mathcal{F}_{\text{par}}$, since
		$Y^{-1}(\{1\}) = \{2,4,6\}$ and $Y^{-1}(\{0\}) = \{1,3,5\}$. This is the precise sense
		in which measurability is the licence of an instrument: a sigma algebra encodes how much
		an experimenter can resolve, and the observables are exactly the functions that do not
		demand a finer resolution than that.

		The law of $X$ is obtained from the push forward \eqref{eq:PushForward}. For a Borel set
		$B\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ we have $X^{-1}(B) = B\cap\Omega$, therefore
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:DieLaw}
			P_{X}(B) = \frac{\left|B\cap\{1,\dots,6\}\right|}{6}
			= \frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=1}^{6}\delta_{k}(B),
		\end{equation}
		where $\delta_{k}(B)$ is equal to one when $k\in B$ and to zero otherwise. This measure
		lives on six points, a set of zero Lebesgue measure, and it is not absolutely
		continuous with respect to $\lambda$: the interval $[5.9,6.1]$ has probability $1/6$
		although its length can be made as small as we please by shrinking it around $6$. The
		die possesses no probability density, and it is the same obstruction that we met for
		the ideal laser, a law concentrated on a set that the Lebesgue measure does not see.

		The expectation \eqref{eq:Expectation} is an integral against $P$, and on a finite space
		an integral is a finite sum,
		\begin{equation*}
			E\{X\} = \int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega) = \sum_{k=1}^{6}k\,P(\{k\})
			= \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = \frac{7}{2}.
		\end{equation*}
		The value $7/2$ is not a possible result of any throw, which makes visible the warning
		that an average is a property of the measure and not of any single realisation. The
		second moment and the variance follow in the same way,
		\begin{equation*}
			E\left\{X^{2}\right\} = \frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}{6} = \frac{91}{6},
			\qquad
			E\left\{X^{2}\right\} - E\{X\}^{2} = \frac{91}{6} - \frac{49}{4}
			= \frac{182-147}{12} = \frac{35}{12},
		\end{equation*}
		and since $E\{X^{2}\}<\infty$ the face value belongs to $L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$,
		so that the whole Hilbert space structure \eqref{eq:L2InnerProduct} is available for it.

	\subsection{Interference with a beam of random phase}

		The second example is closer to the laboratory and was chosen because it contains, in a
		few lines, the reason why two independent lamps do not produce interference fringes.
		Consider a fixed point of a screen illuminated by two scalar, monochromatic beams of the
		same frequency and the same polarisation. The first one is a reference beam of real
		amplitude $E_{r}>0$ and fixed phase, which we take as the origin of phases. The second
		one has got real amplitude $E_{0}>0$ and a phase $\varphi$ that is completely
		uncontrolled, for instance because it comes from a laser whose cavity length drifts by
		many wavelengths between two measurements. All we know about $\varphi$ is that no value
		is preferred to any other.

		The elementary outcome is one value of the phase, so the sample space is the interval
		$\Omega = [0,2\pi)$, and here, unlike the die, we cannot take every subset as an event,
		so we take the Borel sigma algebra $\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{B}([0,2\pi))$. The statement
		``no phase is preferred'' fixes the measure: the probability of finding the phase in an
		interval must depend only on its length and not on its position, which is the
		translation invariance that defines the Lebesgue measure, and the normalisation then
		requires
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:RandomPhaseMeasure}
			P(A) = \frac{\lambda(A)}{2\pi}, \qquad A\in\mathcal{B}([0,2\pi)).
		\end{equation}
		Here, for the first time, both measures of this section appear in the same formula, and
		the uniform probability is simply the Lebesgue measure normalised. One consequence is
		worth pointing out at once. A single point has got zero length, so
		$P(\{\varphi_{0}\}) = 0$ for every $\varphi_{0}$, and yet in every realisation some phase
		does occur: an event of probability zero is not an impossible event, it is an event
		whose chance is not detectable by any finite sequence of trials. This is the price of
		an uncountable $\Omega$ and the reason why probabilities on a continuum are assigned to
		intervals and never to points.

		The total field on the screen is the random variable
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:RandomPhaseField}
			E(\varphi) = E_{r} + E_{0}e^{i\varphi},
		\end{equation}
		measurable because it is continuous, and the intensity read by the detector is
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:RandomPhaseIntensity}
			\begin{split}
				I(\varphi) = \left|E(\varphi)\right|^{2}
				&= \left(E_{r} + E_{0}e^{i\varphi}\right)\left(E_{r} + E_{0}e^{-i\varphi}\right)\\
				&= E_{r}^{2} + E_{0}^{2} + E_{r}E_{0}\left(e^{i\varphi} + e^{-i\varphi}\right)\\
				&= I_{0} + V\cos\varphi,
			\end{split}
		\end{equation}
		where we have written $I_{0} = E_{r}^{2}+E_{0}^{2}$ for the sum of the two intensities
		and $V = 2E_{r}E_{0}$ for the amplitude of the interference term. In every single
		realisation the interference term is there, untouched, and the reading lies somewhere
		in the interval $[I_{0}-V,\,I_{0}+V]$.

		The expectation of the intensity can be computed in the two ways allowed by
		\eqref{eq:Expectation}, and doing both is the best way of seeing that they are one
		definition. On the sample space, using \eqref{eq:RandomPhaseMeasure},
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:RandomPhaseMean}
			E\{I\} = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(I_{0} + V\cos\varphi\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi}
			= I_{0}\,\frac{2\pi}{2\pi} + \frac{V}{2\pi}\textcolor{red}{\cancel{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi}}
			= I_{0},
		\end{equation}
		because the cosine integrates to $\sin 2\pi - \sin 0 = 0$ over a whole period. The
		interference term has disappeared, and with the same computation the mean of the field
		itself is $E\{E\} = E_{r} + \frac{E_{0}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi = E_{r}$,
		that is, the random beam contributes nothing to the average amplitude although it
		contributes the full $E_{0}^{2}$ to the average intensity.

		For the second way we need the law of $I$, which is the push forward of
		\eqref{eq:RandomPhaseMeasure} by \eqref{eq:RandomPhaseIntensity}, and it is the
		calculation that best shows the push forward at work. Take $s\in[I_{0}-V,\,I_{0}+V]$ and
		\textcolor{blue}{introduce the auxiliary variable $u = (s-I_{0})/V\in[-1,1]$}. The
		distribution function of $I$ is the probability of the event ``the reading does not
		exceed $s$'', which is the preimage of the half line $(-\infty,s]$,
		\begin{equation*}
			F_{I}(s) = P_{I}\left((-\infty,s]\right) = P\left(\left\{\varphi : I_{0} + V\cos\varphi\leq s\right\}\right)
			= P\left(\left\{\varphi : \cos\varphi\leq u\right\}\right).
		\end{equation*}
		On $[0,2\pi)$ the cosine is not larger than $u$ precisely on the interval between
		$\arccos u$ and $2\pi - \arccos u$, whose length is $2\pi - 2\arccos u$, so by
		\eqref{eq:RandomPhaseMeasure}
		\begin{equation*}
			F_{I}(s) = \frac{2\pi - 2\arccos u}{2\pi} = 1 - \frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\left(\frac{s-I_{0}}{V}\right).
		\end{equation*}
		This function is differentiable inside the interval, so the law is absolutely
		continuous and its density is obtained by the chain rule, with
		$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\arccos u = -1/\sqrt{1-u^{2}}$ and $\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}s = 1/V$,
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:ArcsineLaw}
			p_{I}(s) = \frac{\mathrm{d}F_{I}}{\mathrm{d}s}
			= \frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{V}
			= \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{V^{2}-\left(s-I_{0}\right)^{2}}},
			\qquad I_{0}-V< s< I_{0}+V,
		\end{equation}
		and $p_{I}(s) = 0$ outside. As a check, the substitution $s - I_{0} = V\sin\theta$ gives
		$\int_{I_{0}-V}^{I_{0}+V}p_{I}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\mathrm{d}\theta = 1$,
		and since \eqref{eq:ArcsineLaw} is symmetric about $I_{0}$ the integral
		$\int s\,p_{I}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ returns $I_{0}$, in agreement with \eqref{eq:RandomPhaseMean}.
		The two members of \eqref{eq:Expectation} have been computed on two different spaces,
		$[0,2\pi)$ with the uniform measure and the real line with the arcsine density, and they
		agree, as they must.

		The physical reading of \eqref{eq:ArcsineLaw} is instructive and somewhat surprising.
		The density is smallest at the mean value $I_{0}$ and diverges at the two ends of the
		interval, so if we repeat the experiment many times and draw the histogram of the
		readings, the detector will most often report values close to a bright fringe or close
		to a dark fringe, and rarely the average. The reason is that the cosine lingers near its
		turning points and crosses its zero quickly, so a uniform phase spends more of its
		measure where $\cos\varphi$ is close to $\pm1$. This leads to the lesson of the example:
		\textbf{the interference is not destroyed in any single realisation, it is destroyed by
		the average over the probability measure}. A fast detector that reads one realisation
		at a time sees fringes whose position wanders from shot to shot; an observer who averages
		over many realisations sees the flat intensity $I_{0} = E_{r}^{2}+E_{0}^{2}$, the plain sum of the
		intensities of two incoherent beams. Whether the averaging is done over the ensemble, as here, or over the
		response time of a slow detector, as in the chapter on interference, is precisely the
		question of ergodicity to which we return at the end of this section.

	\subsection{Stochastic processes}

		A single random variable describes the field at one point of spacetime. To describe it
		everywhere at once we need a family.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Stochastic process)} Let $T$ be an index set. A \emph{stochastic
			process} is a family $\{X_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in T}$ of random variables defined on a
			common probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$, equivalently a map
			$X:T\times\Omega\to S$ such that $X(\tau,\cdot)$ is measurable for every $\tau\in T$.
		\end{myDef}

		A stochastic process admits two readings, and the whole intuition of the subject lies in
		holding both at once \citep{doob1953stochastic}. If we fix the index $\tau$ and let
		$\omega$ vary, we obtain a random variable: a \textit{vertical} slice through
		Figure~\ref{fig:randomvar}, comparing all realisations at one instant. If instead we fix
		$\omega$ and let $\tau$ vary, we obtain a single function of the index, called a
		\emph{sample path} or \emph{realisation}: a \textit{horizontal} slice, that is, one
		of the individual curves of the figure. The experimentalist observes horizontal slices
		and the theory speaks about vertical ones, and reconciling the two is the central
		practical problem of statistical optics.

	\subsection{The probability space of the electromagnetic field}

		We can now carry out the construction that this whole section was preparing, and build
		explicitly the probability space on which coherence theory operates. Let
		$D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}$ be the region of spacetime in which we intend
		to describe the field. Before saying what the points of the sample space are we have to
		say what kind of object the field is, and this requires recalling two notions that run
		underneath the whole of statistical optics, the signal and the analytic signal.

		A \textit{signal} is, for our purposes, a physical quantity read as a function of time
		at a fixed place,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:Signal}
			\begin{split}
				u^{(r)} : \mathbb{R} &\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}\\
				t &\longmapsto u^{(r)}(t).
			\end{split}
		\end{equation}
		The voltage across a photodiode is a signal, and so is one Cartesian component of the
		electric field at a fixed point $\vec{r}$. The superscript $(r)$ reminds us that the
		quantity is real, since every number an instrument returns is real. When the source is
		deterministic there is a single such function; when the source is random, every
		realisation $\omega$ of the previous subsections supplies its own signal, and a
		\textit{random signal} is nothing but a stochastic process indexed by time whose sample
		paths are the individual curves of Figure~\ref{fig:randomvar}.

		The spectral content of a signal is read from its Fourier transform, which with the
		sign convention of these notes is
		$\tilde{u}(\nu) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{(r)}(t)\,e^{i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t$. Since
		$u^{(r)}$ is real, conjugating this integral only flips the sign of the exponent, and
		therefore

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:RealSymmetry}
			\tilde{u}^{*}(\nu) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{(r)}(t)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t = \tilde{u}(-\nu).
		\end{equation}
		The negative frequencies of a real signal carry no information that the positive ones
		do not already carry: half of the spectrum is redundant. Dennis Gabor drew the obvious
		conclusion in 1946, in the paper that founded the modern theory of communication
		\citep{gabor1946theory}: throw the negative frequencies away and keep only the
		positive ones.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Analytic signal)} The \emph{analytic signal} associated with the real
			signal $u^{(r)}$ is
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:AnalyticSignal}
				u(t) = 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(\nu)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu .
			\end{equation}
		\end{myDef}

		The factor two is there so that nothing is lost. Splitting the inverse transform of the
		real signal at $\nu=0$, substituting $\nu\to-\nu$ in the negative half and using
		\eqref{eq:RealSymmetry} in it,

		\begin{align}
			u^{(r)}(t) &= \int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(\nu)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu + \int_{-\infty}^{0}\tilde{u}(\nu)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu \nonumber\\
			&= \int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(\nu)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu + \int_{0}^{\infty}\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{u}^{*}(\nu)}\,e^{i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu \nonumber\\
			&= \frac{1}{2}\left[u(t) + u^{*}(t)\right] = \operatorname{Re}\,u(t),
		\end{align}
		so the physical signal is recovered as the real part of the analytic one. Its imaginary
		part is not independent information either; it is fixed by the real part through the
		Hilbert transform \citep{mandel1995optical},
		$u^{(i)}(t) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{P}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u^{(r)}(t')}{t'-t}\,\mathrm{d}t'$,
		where $\mathrm{P}$ denotes the Cauchy principal value. The name comes from the fact that,
		containing only factors $e^{-i2\pi\nu t}$ with $\nu>0$, the function $u(t)$ can be
		continued to complex values of $t$ and is analytic in the whole lower half plane, where
		every such factor decays.

		The simplest example says almost everything. For $u^{(r)}(t) = A\cos(2\pi\nu_{0}t)$ the
		spectrum consists of two deltas at $\pm\nu_{0}$, the definition \eqref{eq:AnalyticSignal}
		keeps only the one at $+\nu_{0}$, and $u(t) = A\,e^{-i2\pi\nu_{0}t}$. This is the complex
		exponential with which every wave of the previous chapters was written, so that
		\textbf{the analytic signal is the generalisation of the phasor to light that is not
		monochromatic}. It also explains why optics works with it: $|u|^{2}=A^{2}$ is constant,
		whereas $(u^{(r)})^{2}=A^{2}\cos^{2}(2\pi\nu_{0}t)$ oscillates at $2\nu_{0}$, some
		$10^{15}$ times per second, a rate no detector can follow. The modulus squared of the
		analytic signal discards exactly the oscillation that the detector cannot see and keeps
		the envelope that it does read. Finally, keeping the positive frequencies is a linear
		operation that commutes with derivatives in space and time, so if the real field solves
		Maxwell's equations its analytic signal solves them too. This is why, from now on, the
		field is the complex, three component object
		$\vec{\mathcal{E}} = (\mathcal{E}_{x},\mathcal{E}_{y},\mathcal{E}_{z})$ whose real part is
		the physical field.

		As sample space we take

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:FieldSampleSpace}
			\Omega = \left\{\omega : D\to\mathbb{C}^{3} \;\middle|\; \omega \text{ satisfies Maxwell's equations in } D\right\},
		\end{equation}
		that is, the set of admissible field histories. The restriction is essential and it is
		physical: nature does not select arbitrary functions of spacetime but only those
		compatible with the dynamics. Randomness enters through the sources and never
		through the propagation, which remains exactly as deterministic as it was in the
		previous chapters. A probability measure on an optical field is a measure on the
		solution space of Maxwell's equations, and this is why, as we shall see in a later
		subsection, coherence itself obeys a wave equation.

		With this choice the field becomes the simplest map imaginable, namely the evaluation of
		a function at a point,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:EvaluationMap}
			\mathcal{E}_{j}(\vec{r},t) : \Omega\to\mathbb{C}, \qquad \omega\mapsto\omega_{j}(\vec{r},t),
			\qquad j\in\{x,y,z\},
		\end{equation}
		so that for each spacetime point and each Cartesian component the field is a complex
		random variable, and the vector $\vec{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},t):\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{3}$
		is a $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ valued random variable. The whole field is the stochastic process
		indexed by $T = D\times\{x,y,z\}$.

		We equip $\Omega$ with the \emph{cylinder sigma algebra}
		$\mathcal{F}$, generated by the sets

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CylinderSets}
			C = \left\{\omega\in\Omega \;\middle|\; \left(\omega(\vec{r}_{1},t_{1}),\dots,\omega(\vec{r}_{n},t_{n})\right)\in B\right\},
			\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{3n}),
		\end{equation}
		for every finite collection of spacetime points and every Borel set $B$.

		The physical reading of \eqref{eq:CylinderSets} is the most important remark of this
		section. A cylinder set is exactly a statement that can be checked by placing a
		finite number of detectors at a finite number of spacetime points and looking at their
		readings. No experiment has ever measured a field everywhere; every experiment samples
		it at finitely many places and finitely many instants. The sigma algebra generated by
		the cylinder sets is therefore the collection of all statements decidable by countable
		sequences of finite measurements, which is to say, the collection of all statements an
		optical experiment can ever settle. The cylinder construction is not a mathematical
		convenience, it is the transcription of experimental practice.

		It remains to place a probability measure on this enormous space,
		and it would be hopeless to attempt it directly. Kolmogorov's extension theorem
		\citep{kolmogorov1933grundbegriffe} shows that it is unnecessary.

		\begin{myteo}
			\textit{(Kolmogorov extension theorem)} Given a family of finite dimensional
			distributions $P_{(\vec{r}_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(\vec{r}_{n},t_{n})}$ on
			$\mathbb{C}^{3n}$, one for every finite collection of spacetime points, which is
			consistent under permutation of the points and under marginalisation over any of
			them, there exists a unique probability measure $P$ on the cylinder sigma algebra
			$\mathcal{F}$ whose finite dimensional marginals are the given ones.
		\end{myteo}

		The physical content is emancipating. To specify a source it is enough to
		specify the joint statistics of any finite set of detector readings, and this is
		precisely the data that an experiment can access. We never have to say what $P$ does to
		the monstrous space of all field histories; we say what the joint distribution of two
		detectors is, and of three, and so on, taking care only that these prescriptions do not
		contradict one another, and Kolmogorov guarantees that they knit together into one
		consistent object. The probability space of an optical field is built out of exactly the
		information a laboratory can supply.

		We assume finally, as a finite energy hypothesis, that

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:FiniteEnergy}
			E\left\{\left|\mathcal{E}_{j}(\vec{r},t)\right|^{2}\right\}<\infty
			\qquad \text{for every } j \text{ and every } (\vec{r},t)\in D,
		\end{equation}
		which is the statement that the mean energy density is finite, that is, that the source
		does not radiate infinite power. Physically it is unobjectionable, and mathematically it
		is exactly the hypothesis that places every field component inside the Hilbert space
		\eqref{eq:L2Omega}. From this point on, every component of the field at every point of
		spacetime is a vector in one and the same Hilbert space, and the geometry of that space
		is the geometry of coherence.

		We may now define, with every symbol having been given a
		meaning, the object that the rest of this chapter studies. For two spacetime points and
		two Cartesian components,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CoherenceTensor}
			\Gamma_{jk}(\vec{r},t;\vec{r}\,',t') = E\left\{\mathcal{E}_{j}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}(\vec{r}\,',t')\right\}
			= \braket{\mathcal{E}_{j}(\vec{r},t),\mathcal{E}_{k}(\vec{r}\,',t')},
		\end{equation}
		a $3\times3$ matrix attached to every pair of spacetime points, the inner product being
		the one of \eqref{eq:L2InnerProduct}. This is the vector generalisation of the
		correlation function \eqref{eq:CorrFunc}, and three of its properties follow at once
		from the construction.

		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Hermitian symmetry: exchanging the two points and the two indices and
			conjugating,
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:GammaHermitian}
				\Gamma_{kj}^{*}(\vec{r}\,',t';\vec{r},t) = E\left\{\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}(\vec{r}\,',t')\,\mathcal{E}_{j}(\vec{r},t)\right\} = \Gamma_{jk}(\vec{r},t;\vec{r}\,',t'),
			\end{equation}
			since conjugation may be taken inside the expectation, which is an integral of a
			complex function.
			\item Non negative definiteness: for any finite set of spacetime points
			labelled by $m$ and any complex coefficients $a_{j}^{(m)}$,
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:GammaPositive}
				\sum_{m,n}\sum_{j,k}a_{j}^{(m)}a_{k}^{(n)*}\,\Gamma_{jk}(m;n)
				= E\left\{\left|\sum_{m}\sum_{j}a_{j}^{(m)}\mathcal{E}_{j}(m)\right|^{2}\right\}\geq 0,
			\end{equation}
			because the expectation of a non negative quantity is non negative. Physically this
			says that no superposition of field components, at whatever points, can carry
			negative intensity.
			\item Bounded correlation: by \eqref{eq:CauchySchwarzProb},
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:GammaBound}
				\left|\Gamma_{jk}(\vec{r},t;\vec{r}\,',t')\right|^{2} \leq \Gamma_{jj}(\vec{r},t;\vec{r},t)\;\Gamma_{kk}(\vec{r}\,',t';\vec{r}\,',t'),
			\end{equation}
			which is what allows the normalised degree of coherence to be confined to the
			interval $[0,1]$.
		\end{enumerate}

		It is worth noticing where polarisation fits into this. Evaluating \eqref{eq:CoherenceTensor} at a single spacetime point,
		$\vec{r}=\vec{r}\,'$ and $t=t'$, the diagonal entries $\Gamma_{jj}$ give the mean
		intensity carried by each Cartesian component while the off diagonal entries measure the
		correlation between components, and the resulting matrix is exactly the polarisation
		matrix \eqref{eq:CoherenceMatrix} introduced in the chapter on polarisation.
		Polarisation is coherence between the components of the field at one point;
		coherence proper is correlation between the field at different points. They are the
		same tensor read in two different regimes, which is the reason a unified treatment of
		coherence and polarisation is possible at all \citep{wolf2007introduction}.

	\subsection{Stationarity, ergodicity and the right to measure}

		One question remains, and it is the question on which the experimental relevance of
		everything above depends. The expectation \eqref{eq:Expectation} is an average over
		$\Omega$, and $\Omega$ is not accessible: nature hands us one realisation. What we can
		average over is time, along that single realisation. Under what conditions do the two
		agree?

		The first condition concerns the invariance of the source.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Stationarity)} Let $\theta_{\tau}:\Omega\to\Omega$ be the time shift
			defined by $(\theta_{\tau}\omega)(\vec{r},t) = \omega(\vec{r},t+\tau)$. The field is
			\textbf{stationary} if $P$ is invariant under every such shift,
			\begin{equation}\label{eq:StationarityMeasure}
				P\left(\theta_{\tau}^{-1}A\right) = P(A) \qquad \text{for all } A\in\mathcal{F} \text{ and all } \tau\in\mathbb{R}.
			\end{equation}
		\end{myDef}

		This is the measure theoretic form of the statement, given in a more elementary way in
		the next section, that the statistical properties of the light do not depend on when we
		choose to start the clock. A lamp switched on an hour ago and left alone is stationary; a
		pulsed source is not. Its immediate consequence is that the coherence tensor
		\eqref{eq:CoherenceTensor} can depend on the two instants only through their difference,
		$\Gamma_{jk}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,';t,t') = \Gamma_{jk}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,';\tau)$ with
		$\tau=t'-t$, which is what makes the Wiener-Khinchin theorem of the following section
		possible at all.

		The second condition is stronger and concerns the indecomposability of the source.

		\begin{myDef}
			\textit{(Ergodicity)} A stationary field is \textbf{ergodic} if every shift
			invariant event is trivial, that is, if $\theta_{\tau}^{-1}A = A$ for all $\tau$
			implies $P(A)=0$ or $P(A)=1$.
		\end{myDef}

		Physically, ergodicity says that the source cannot be split into two sub ensembles that
		never communicate. If it could, a single realisation would remain trapped in one of them
		forever and could never report on the other. When it holds, Birkhoff's ergodic theorem
		\citep{birkhoff1931proof} applies and gives the result we need,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ergodic}
			\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{j}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}(\vec{r}\,',t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}t
			= E\left\{\mathcal{E}_{j}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}(\vec{r}\,',t+\tau)\right\}
		\end{equation}
		for almost every realisation $\omega$.

		Equation \eqref{eq:Ergodic} is the licence, and the only licence, to replace the
		inaccessible ensemble average by the accessible time average of a single beam, and it
		is the reason the whole theory is experimentally meaningful rather than merely elegant.
		Notice, finally, what the two sides of \eqref{eq:Ergodic} are: on the left an integral
		against the Lebesgue measure on the time axis, on the right an integral against the
		probability measure on $\Omega$. The two measures we were at pains to keep apart at the
		beginning of this section meet here, in a single identity, and ergodicity is precisely
		the hypothesis under which they may be exchanged. With that hypothesis in hand we may
		return to physics and define the coherence function itself.

\section{The coherence function}

	The concept of the coherence function is, at the mathematical level, identical to that of
	the correlation function given in equation \eqref{eq:CorrFunc}; both represent
	correlations. However, coherence is a term widely used in the study of electromagnetic
	radiation; let us see a little of why this is so.

	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{Chapter 6/Figures/RandomVar}
		\caption{Representation of $E(\vec{r},t)$ as a random variable.}
		\label{fig:randomvar}
	\end{figure}

	In Figure~\ref{fig:randomvar} we observe a component of the electric field, represented by
	$E(\vec{r},t)$, seen across several observations or realisations. Behaving as a random
	variable, in each realisation it has a different behaviour in time at a point $\vec{r}$;
	in general, here we have indexed $^{(i)}E(\vec{r},t)$ with integers, but in general there
	could be an uncountable set of possible states for the electric field. If we look at
	Figure~\ref{fig:randomvar} there are two temporal points, $t,t'$, at which we isolate
	parts of these random oscillations; we could ask how correlated these oscillations are
	across the realisations over the interval $t'-t$, or in other words, how
	\textit{coherent} the oscillations are across the realisations.

	We speak of coherence when we want to analyse the correlation of electromagnetic radiation
	between spacetime points, and after the previous section every ingredient of that
	sentence has a precise meaning. The field component $\mathcal{E}$ (for the moment a
	single one, so that the field is understood as scalar) is the analytic signal
	\eqref{eq:AnalyticSignal} of the real field; at each spacetime point it is the evaluation
	map \eqref{eq:EvaluationMap}, a complex random variable on the probability space
	$(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ of admissible field histories; and the finite energy hypothesis
	\eqref{eq:FiniteEnergy} places it in the Hilbert space $L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ of
	\eqref{eq:L2Omega}. With all of this in hand the coherence function can be written down
	again, this time without a single undefined symbol.

	\begin{myDef}
		\textit{(Coherence function)} Let $\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$
		for every $(\vec{r},t)\in D$. The \emph{coherence function} of the field between the
		spacetime points $(\vec{r},t)$ and $(\vec{r}\,',t')$ is
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CoherenceFunction}
			\Gamma(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,';t,t') = E\left\{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(\vec{r}\,',t')\right\}
			= \int_{\Omega}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r}\,',t')\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)
			= \braket{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t),\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}\,',t')}.
		\end{equation}
	\end{myDef}

	Each of the three expressions in \eqref{eq:CoherenceFunction} says something. The first is
	the ensemble average of the old notation. The second, obtained by inserting the
	evaluation map into the definition \eqref{eq:Expectation} of the expectation, says what
	that average actually does: it runs over every admissible field history $\omega$, reads
	the value of that history at the two spacetime points, multiplies one by the conjugate of
	the other and weighs the product with the probability $P$ that the source assigns to that
	history. It is a vertical slice through Figure~\ref{fig:randomvar}, taken at two instants
	at once. The third expression, the inner product \eqref{eq:L2InnerProduct}, says that
	$\Gamma$ measures how parallel the two random variables are as vectors of a Hilbert
	space, and it also guarantees that the definition makes sense, because by the
	Cauchy-Schwarz inequality \eqref{eq:CauchySchwarzProb} the integral is bounded by
	$\sqrt{E\{|\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)|^{2}\}\,E\{|\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}\,',t')|^{2}\}}$, which is
	finite precisely because of \eqref{eq:FiniteEnergy}. That was the reason for imposing it.
	Definition \eqref{eq:CoherenceFunction} is the diagonal entry $\Gamma_{jj}$ of the
	coherence tensor \eqref{eq:CoherenceTensor} for the chosen component.

	If we take $\vec{r}=\vec{r}\,'$ we compare the field with itself at two different
	instants and obtain

	\begin{equation}\label{eq:SelfCoherence}
		\Gamma(\vec{r};t,t') = E \left\{ \mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)\, \mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r},t') \right\},
	\end{equation}
	which in these notes we shall keep calling the \textit{mutual coherence function} at the
	point $\vec{r}$, although the reader should be warned that Mandel and Wolf reserve that
	name for the two point object \eqref{eq:CoherenceFunction} and call
	\eqref{eq:SelfCoherence} the \textit{self coherence function}
	\citep{mandel1995optical}.

	We can go even further by including the concept of \textit{statistical stationarity}, but
	one must be careful since there are several kinds. Formally, a stochastic process
	$\{X_t\}$ is considered stationary when its statistical properties are invariant under
	time translations. This condition manifests in different degrees:

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Strict (strong) stationarity: the most demanding form of stationarity requires that all joint probability distributions be time-invariant:

		\begin{equation}
			F_X(x_{t_1+\tau}, \ldots, x_{t_k+\tau}) = F_X(x_{t_1}, \ldots, x_{t_k}) \quad \forall \tau, k, t_i,
		\end{equation}
		where $F_X$ is the joint distribution function. This implies that:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item all moments (mean, variance, skewness, etc.) are constant;
			\item the marginal distributions are identical at any instant;
			\item it is a sufficient but not necessary condition for ARMA models.
		\end{itemize}

		\item Weak (second-order) stationarity: more practical in applications, it only requires invariance of the first two moments:
		\begin{equation}
			\begin{cases}
				\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu \quad (\text{constant}) \quad \forall t \\
				\text{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2 \quad (\text{constant}) \quad \forall t \\
				\text{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \gamma(h) \quad (\text{depends only on } h, \text{ not on } t)
			\end{cases}
		\end{equation}
		This is the definition used in most econometric models (ARIMA, GARCH) and in the Wiener-Khinchin spectral analysis (which we shall use later).

		\item Trend stationarity (deterministic stationarity): a non-stationary process that can be made stationary by removing deterministic components:
		\begin{equation}
			X_t = \alpha + \beta t + \epsilon_t,
		\end{equation}
		where $\epsilon_t$ is stationary. The non-stationarity here comes exclusively from the temporal trend $\beta t$.
	\end{enumerate}

	We shall use a hypothesis of strong stationarity (although weak stationarity often
	suffices), and in the language of the previous section it is exactly the invariance
	\eqref{eq:StationarityMeasure} of the measure. Strict stationarity asks the finite
	dimensional distributions to be unchanged by a time shift, and by the uniqueness part of
	Kolmogorov's extension theorem those distributions determine $P$ completely, so the
	shifted measure $P\circ\theta_{\tau}^{-1}$ and $P$ coincide. Weak stationarity, in turn,
	is all that the coherence function needs, since $\Gamma$ only involves second moments.

	The dependence on the time difference follows from this invariance in one line of
	measure theory. For any integrable random variable $X$, the change of variables formula
	applied to the map $\theta_{s}$ gives

	\begin{equation}
		E\left\{X\circ\theta_{s}\right\} = \int_{\Omega}X\big(\theta_{s}\omega\big)\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)
		= \int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\big(\textcolor{blue}{P\circ\theta_{s}^{-1}}\big)(\omega)
		= \int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\textcolor{blue}{P}(\omega) = E\{X\},
	\end{equation}
	where in the third step we used \eqref{eq:StationarityMeasure}. Taking
	$X(\omega) = \omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t')$, the shifted variable is
	$X(\theta_{s}\omega) = \omega(\vec{r},t+s)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t'+s)$, and choosing the
	shift $s=-t$ we arrive at

	\begin{equation}\label{eq:GammaTau}
		\Gamma(\vec{r};t,t') = E\left\{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},0)\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(\vec{r},t'-t)\right\}
		= \Gamma(\vec{r};\tau), \qquad \tau = t'-t .
	\end{equation}
	As we can see, the mutual coherence at a point $\vec{r}$ under a stationarity hypothesis
	depends only on the delay $\tau$ between the two instants. Physically, the source has no
	clock: it cannot tell whether we start comparing its field at noon or at midnight, only
	how long we wait between the two comparisons. Throughout what follows
	$\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) = E\{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)\}$, with
	any $t$.

	It is worth asking: how necessary is it to analyse the ensemble average; why not take the
	temporal average? Answering this in general is very complicated, since it touches on the
	problem of \textit{ergodicity}. Ergodicity, in the sense of ergodic theory, requires that
	a dynamical system satisfy that the temporal averages coincide with the ensemble averages
	for almost every initial condition. Its rigorous proof is highly non trivial, to the point
	that the problems for which ergodicity has been proven with mathematical rigour are few.

	From a phenomenological standpoint we shall assume, from now on, an \textit{ergodic
	hypothesis} in which electromagnetic radiation has a stationary and ergodic character in
	the sense of the definitions of the previous section. Under these conditions Birkhoff's
	theorem \eqref{eq:Ergodic}, with $j=k$ and $\vec{r}=\vec{r}\,'$, states that the mutual
	coherence function coincides with the temporal average along a single realisation,

	\begin{equation}\label{eq:GammaTimeAverage}
		\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} \omega(\vec{r},t)\, \omega^*(\vec{r},t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}t
		\qquad \text{for } P \text{ almost every } \omega .
	\end{equation}
	The left hand side is an integral over $\Omega$ against $P$ and the right hand side an
	integral over the time axis against the Lebesgue measure, and this identity is what
	allows us, from here on, to write $\braket{\cdots}$ for either of the two averages
	without ambiguity.

	The mutual coherence function has several properties, some of which are:

	\begin{itemize}
		\item[a)] $\Gamma(\vec{r}',0) \geq 0$. This follows from the definition, since $\Gamma(\vec{r}',0) = \braket{|\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}')|^2}$, i.e.\ it corresponds to the intensity of the electric field and can only be zero when trivially the field is zero at every instant.

		\item[b)] $\Gamma(\vec{r}',-\tau) = \Gamma^*(\vec{r}',\tau)$. This is known as the \textit{Hermitian property} of the mutual coherence function, and follows from the fact that $\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',t)$ is stationary, which allows arbitrary temporal translations from the origin. In this spirit,

		\begin{align}
			& \Gamma(\vec{r}',\tau) = \braket{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}')\mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r}',t + \tau)} = \braket{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',t-\tau)\mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r}')} = \Gamma^*(\vec{r}',-\tau).
		\end{align}

		\item[c)] $|\Gamma(\vec{r}',\tau)| \leq \Gamma(\vec{r}',0)$. This property follows from the \textit{Cauchy-Schwarz inequality}

		\begin{equation}
			|\Gamma(\vec{r}',\tau)|^2 = | \braket{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}')\mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r}',t+\tau)} |^2 \leq  \braket{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',t)\mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r}',t)} \braket{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',t+\tau)\mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r}',t+\tau)} = \Gamma^2(\vec{r}',0),
		\end{equation}

		which implies that $|\Gamma(\vec{r}',\tau)|$ can never exceed its initial value $\Gamma(\vec{r}',0)$. (This property is, let us recall, exclusive to the mutual coherence function; when we consider the coherence between two sources we will see that it is not satisfied - on the contrary, it will increase.)

		\item[d)] $\Gamma(\vec{r}',\tau)$ is non-negative. We can see this from the definition through the following, rather obvious, inequality

		\begin{equation}
			\left\langle \left| \int_{T_1}^{T_2} f(t)\mathcal{E}(t)\,dt \right|^2\right\rangle \geq 0,
		\end{equation}
		where $f(t)$ is an arbitrary function and $T_1,T_2$ are arbitrary times. In this way we obtain the inequality

		\begin{equation}
			\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\int_{T_1}^{T_2} f^*(\vec{r}',t)f(\vec{r}',t')\Gamma(\vec{r}',t'-t)\,dt\,dt' \geq 0.
		\end{equation}

		\item[e)] If the electric field is random, stationary and differentiable, then we can find a velocity $\xi (\vec{r}',t) = \dot{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r}',t)$, which is stationary, random and of zero mean value, and whose mutual coherence function is

		\begin{equation}
			\Gamma_\xi(\vec{r}',\tau) = - \ddot{\Gamma}_\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',\tau).
		\end{equation}

		\item[f)] $\Gamma(\vec{r}',\tau)$ is continuous. This holds even when there are jump discontinuities.
	\end{itemize}

	Finally, it is worth mentioning that there is a quantity known as the complex degree of
	coherence, given by

	\begin{equation}
		\gamma (\vec{r}',\tau) = \frac{\Gamma(\vec{r}',\tau)}{\Gamma(\vec{r}',0)},
	\end{equation}

	and from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have $0 \leq |\gamma(\vec{r}',\tau)| \leq 1$.

	\subsection{Manifestations of coherence: the Wiener-Khinchin theorem}

		The mutual coherence function and the power spectral density are two sides of the same coin
		in the study of light and wave signals. Mutual coherence describes how the fluctuations of
		a luminous field are correlated in space and time, acting as a map that reveals to what
		extent the variations at one point of space are synchronised with those at another, even
		with a certain temporal delay. The power spectral density, in turn, decomposes the power
		of the signal into its frequency components, showing which tones or colours (in the case
		of light) dominate its behaviour. The intimate relation between the two concepts lies in
		the fact that, through mathematical transformations, it is possible to convert the detailed
		information on the spacetime correlations captured by mutual coherence into a precise
		description of how the power is distributed in the frequency domain, and vice versa. This
		link allows researchers to jump between complementary perspectives: analysing interference
		patterns in an interferometer or identifying spectral signatures in a material, all from
		the same fundamental principles.

		We are going to find one of the most important theorems in coherence theory, the
		\textit{Wiener-Khinchin theorem}, proved by Norbert Wiener in 1930 for individual
		functions \citep{wiener1930generalized} and by Aleksandr Khinchin in 1934 for stationary
		random processes \citep{khinchin1934korrelationstheorie}. Before writing a single
		integral we have to decide what it is that we decompose in frequency, because there are
		two candidates, the energy spectral density and the power spectral density, and only
		one of them makes sense for the light we are studying.

		For a signal $u$ of finite energy, that is, $u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$, the theorem of
		Plancherel \citep{plancherel1910contribution} guarantees that the Fourier transform
		$\tilde{u}(\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t)\,e^{i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t$ exists and that

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:ParsevalEnergy}
			\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_{\mathbb{R}}|\tilde{u}(\nu)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu ,
		\end{equation}
		so that $|\tilde{u}(\nu)|^{2}$ is the energy carried per unit frequency interval: the
		\textit{energy spectral density}. This is the right object for a pulse, a field that is
		switched on, does something and is switched off again. It is, however, the wrong object
		for a stationary field, and the reason is the content of the following statement.

		\begin{myteo}
			Let $\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)$ be a stationary field at a fixed point $\vec{r}$ with
			$\Gamma(\vec{r};0)>0$. Then its expected total energy is infinite and, if the field is
			moreover ergodic, almost every realisation $\omega(\vec{r},\cdot)$ belongs neither to
			$L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$ nor to $L^{1}(\mathbb{R})$. In consequence the realisations of a
			stationary field have no Fourier transform as functions, and no energy spectral
			density.
		\end{myteo}

		\textbf{\textit{Demonstration:}} Let
		$W_{T}(\omega)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t$ be the energy
		collected during a window of duration $T$ in the realisation $\omega$. Its expectation is
		an integral over $\Omega$ of an integral over time; since the integrand is non negative,
		Tonelli's theorem allows the order of the two integrations to be exchanged without any
		further condition, and

		\begin{align}
			E\{W_{T}\} &= \int_{\Omega}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)
			= \int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\int_{\Omega}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t \nonumber\\
			&= \int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\textcolor{blue}{\Gamma(\vec{r};0)}\,\mathrm{d}t = T\,\Gamma(\vec{r};0),
		\end{align}
		where we used \eqref{eq:GammaTau}: by stationarity
		$E\{|\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)|^{2}\}=\Gamma(\vec{r};0)$ is the same at every instant. The
		energies $W_{T}$ grow monotonically with $T$ towards the total energy
		$W=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t$, so by the monotone convergence
		theorem $E\{W\}=\lim_{T\to\infty}T\,\Gamma(\vec{r};0)=\infty$, which is the first claim.

		For the second we go from the average to the individual realisation using ergodicity.
		Equation \eqref{eq:GammaTimeAverage} at $\tau=0$ states that, for almost every $\omega$,

		\begin{equation}
			\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{W_{T}(\omega)}{T} = \Gamma(\vec{r};0) > 0 ,
		\end{equation}
		so that, for $T$ large enough, $W_{T}(\omega)\geq\frac{1}{2}\,T\,\Gamma(\vec{r};0)$,
		which grows without bound. Hence $\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty$
		and $\omega(\vec{r},\cdot)\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$. The same argument applied to the
		integrable random variable $|\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)|$, whose mean $E\{|\mathcal{E}|\}$ is
		strictly positive (it could vanish only if $\mathcal{E}=0$ almost surely, which would
		force $\Gamma(\vec{r};0)=0$), gives $\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty$,
		so $\omega(\vec{r},\cdot)\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R})$ either. Now the Fourier integral
		$\int_{\mathbb{R}}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,e^{i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t$ exists as a Lebesgue
		integral only for functions in $L^{1}(\mathbb{R})$, and Plancherel's extension exists
		only for functions in $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$, so neither construction applies. Even if some
		generalised transform were attached to the realisation, \eqref{eq:ParsevalEnergy} would
		force its modulus squared to integrate to $W=\infty$, so it could never be a density of a
		finite quantity. $\blacksquare$

		The physics behind the proof is simple. A stationary source has, by definition, been
		shining since the infinite past and will go on shining forever with the same finite
		intensity $\Gamma(\vec{r};0)$, so the energy it delivers is infinite, and asking how that
		total energy is distributed among the frequencies is asking how to share out infinity.
		The individual realisations of stationary light do possess a spectral decomposition, but
		only in the much weaker sense of Wiener's generalised harmonic analysis
		\citep{wiener1930generalized}, as random measures and not as functions.

		The way out is visible in the proof itself: the energy $W_{T}$ diverges, but it diverges
		linearly in $T$, so the energy per unit time, which is the power, stays finite. The
		quantity to decompose in frequency is therefore the power. We first truncate each
		realisation to a window of duration $T$,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:NearlyWiener}
			^\omega \mathcal{E}_T (\vec{r},t) = \left\{
			\begin{matrix}
				^\omega\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t) & -\frac{T}{2}\leq t \leq \frac{T}{2}\\
				0 & \text{otherwise}
			\end{matrix} \right.
		\end{equation}
		and this truncated signal does belong to $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$ for almost every $\omega$,
		since its energy $W_{T}$ has the finite expectation $T\,\Gamma(\vec{r};0)$ and a non
		negative random variable with finite expectation is finite almost surely. Its Fourier
		transform therefore exists, and its energy spectral density

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:SpecWT}
			^\omega S_T (\vec{r},\nu) = \left| \int_\mathbb{R} {}^\omega \mathcal{E}_T(\vec{r},t)e^{i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t \right|^2 = \left| \int_{-T/2}^{T/2}{}^\omega \mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)e^{i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t \right|^2
		\end{equation}
		is well defined. By \eqref{eq:ParsevalEnergy} it integrates over $\nu$ to $W_{T}$, which
		grows like $T$; dividing by $T$ gives a density whose integral, $W_{T}/T$, tends to the
		finite value $\Gamma(\vec{r};0)$. This motivates the definition of the
		\textit{power spectral density},

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:SpecW}
			S(\vec{r},\nu) = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{E\left\{{}^\omega S_T(\vec{r},\nu)\right\}}{T}.
		\end{equation}
		The order of the operations in \eqref{eq:SpecW}, first the ensemble average and then the
		limit, is not a matter of taste. The quantity ${}^\omega S_{T}/T$ of a single realisation,
		called the periodogram, does not converge as $T\to\infty$: for thermal light its
		fluctuations about the mean remain as large as the mean itself however long we observe
		\citep{goodman2015statistical}, and the spectrum of one realisation is as speckled as a
		laser spot on a rough wall. A spectrometer provides the missing average by integrating
		over its finite resolution bandwidth.

		We now evaluate \eqref{eq:SpecW}, and we shall do it in an order that keeps the
		single realisation in view for as long as possible, because this is what will let us see
		where stationarity acts and where ergodicity acts. Writing the modulus squared in
		\eqref{eq:SpecWT} as the product of the integral by its complex conjugate, and giving the
		conjugated factor its own integration variable $t'$ so that the two integrals do not get
		mixed up,

		\begin{align}
			{}^\omega S_{T}(\vec{r},\nu) &= \left(\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,e^{i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\left(\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\omega(\vec{r},t')\,e^{i2\pi\nu t'}\,\mathrm{d}t'\right)^{*} \nonumber\\
			&= \int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t')\,e^{i2\pi\nu(t-t')}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}t' . \label{eq:SpecWTdouble}
		\end{align}
		The product of the two one dimensional integrals may be written as a double integral
		over the square $Q_{T}=[-T/2,T/2]\times[-T/2,T/2]$ of the $(t,t')$ plane because the
		integrand is absolutely integrable there: $\iint_{Q_{T}}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|\,|\omega(\vec{r},t')|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}t'
		=\left(\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|\omega(\vec{r},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}\leq T\,W_{T}(\omega)$
		by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the window, and $W_{T}(\omega)$ is finite for almost
		every $\omega$, as we have just seen. Fubini's theorem therefore allows us to integrate
		over $Q_{T}$ in any order and with any change of variables we like.

		The integrand of \eqref{eq:SpecWTdouble} involves the two instants $t$ and $t'$
		separately, but the exponential already depends only on their difference, and we shall
		see in a moment that, on average, so does the product of fields. This suggests taking as
		new variables the first instant and the delay,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:ChangeTtau}
			\begin{split}
				\Phi:\; Q_{T} &\longrightarrow R_{T}\\
				(t,t') &\longmapsto (t,\tau) = (t,\,t'-t),
			\end{split}
			\qquad\qquad
			\frac{\partial(t,t')}{\partial(t,\tau)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial t}{\partial t} & \dfrac{\partial t}{\partial \tau}\\[2mm] \dfrac{\partial t'}{\partial t} & \dfrac{\partial t'}{\partial \tau}\end{pmatrix}
			= \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = 1 ,
		\end{equation}
		where we used the inverse map $t'=t+\tau$. The Jacobian is one, so
		$\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}t'=\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\tau$: the map is a shear, which slides
		every horizontal line of the plane without stretching it, and areas are preserved. What
		does change is the shape of the integration domain. Following the four corners of the
		square,

		\begin{equation}
			\left(-\tfrac{T}{2},-\tfrac{T}{2}\right)\mapsto\left(-\tfrac{T}{2},0\right),\quad
			\left(\tfrac{T}{2},-\tfrac{T}{2}\right)\mapsto\left(\tfrac{T}{2},-T\right),\quad
			\left(\tfrac{T}{2},\tfrac{T}{2}\right)\mapsto\left(\tfrac{T}{2},0\right),\quad
			\left(-\tfrac{T}{2},\tfrac{T}{2}\right)\mapsto\left(-\tfrac{T}{2},T\right),
		\end{equation}
		the square $Q_{T}$ becomes the parallelogram $R_{T}$ of the $(t,\tau)$ plane with these
		four vertices, described by the two conditions

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:RegionRT}
			R_{T} = \left\{(t,\tau)\;\middle|\; -\frac{T}{2}\leq t\leq\frac{T}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad -\frac{T}{2}\leq t+\tau\leq\frac{T}{2}\right\}.
		\end{equation}
		The delay ranges over $-T\leq\tau\leq T$, since two instants of a window of duration $T$
		can be at most $T$ apart, and Fubini lets us cut $R_{T}$ into horizontal strips of
		constant $\tau$ and integrate first along each strip. On the strip of height $\tau$ the
		instant $t$ must satisfy both conditions of \eqref{eq:RegionRT}, that is
		$t\geq\max\left(-\tfrac{T}{2},-\tfrac{T}{2}-\tau\right)$ and
		$t\leq\min\left(\tfrac{T}{2},\tfrac{T}{2}-\tau\right)$. For $\tau\geq0$ the binding
		conditions are $t\geq-T/2$ and $t\leq T/2-\tau$, and for $\tau<0$ they are
		$t\geq-T/2-\tau$ and $t\leq T/2$, so that

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:StripLimits}
			t\in I_{T}(\tau) =
			\begin{cases}
				\left[-\frac{T}{2},\;\frac{T}{2}-\tau\right], & 0\leq\tau\leq T,\\[2mm]
				\left[-\frac{T}{2}-\tau,\;\frac{T}{2}\right], & -T\leq\tau<0,
			\end{cases}
			\qquad\qquad
			|I_{T}(\tau)| = T-|\tau| .
		\end{equation}
		The length of the strip is $T-|\tau|$ in both cases, which is the elementary fact that
		inside a window of duration $T$ the pairs of instants separated by $\tau$ can only be
		formed during a time $T-|\tau|$; the strips shrink linearly from the full window at
		$\tau=0$ to nothing at $\tau=\pm T$. Rewriting \eqref{eq:SpecWTdouble} in the new
		variables, with $t'=t+\tau$ and $e^{i2\pi\nu(t-t')}=e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}$, and dividing by
		$T$,

		\begin{align}
			\frac{{}^\omega S_{T}(\vec{r},\nu)}{T}
			&= \frac{1}{T}\iint_{R_{T}}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\tau \nonumber\\
			&= \int_{-T}^{T}\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_{I_{T}(\tau)}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau
			\equiv \int_{-T}^{T}{}^{\omega}C_{T}(\vec{r};\tau)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau , \label{eq:PeriodogramCorrelogram}
		\end{align}
		where the exponential has left the inner integral because it does not depend on $t$.
		This is still an exact statement about one realisation, with no average and no
		hypothesis on the source. The bracket ${}^{\omega}C_{T}(\vec{r};\tau)$ multiplies the
		field by its own conjugate a delay $\tau$ later and accumulates the product while
		sliding along the record: it is the correlation of the beam with itself measured by
		time integration, which is exactly what a Michelson interferometer with path difference
		$C\tau$ builds up during an exposure of duration $T$. The periodogram of a single
		realisation is the Fourier transform of this measured correlation.

		We can now follow the coherence function as each hypothesis is switched on. Taking the
		expectation of the bracket, $E$ may be carried inside the $t$ integral by Fubini's
		theorem, since by \eqref{eq:CauchySchwarzProb} and \eqref{eq:FiniteEnergy}
		$E\{|\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)|\}\leq\Gamma(\vec{r};t,t)^{1/2}\,\Gamma(\vec{r};t+\tau,t+\tau)^{1/2}$,
		which for a stationary field is the constant $\Gamma(\vec{r};0)$, integrable over the
		bounded interval $I_{T}(\tau)$. With no hypothesis at all the expectation produces the
		two time coherence function \eqref{eq:SelfCoherence},

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CTnoHyp}
			E\left\{{}^{\omega}C_{T}(\vec{r};\tau)\right\} = \frac{1}{T}\int_{I_{T}(\tau)}E\left\{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)\right\}\mathrm{d}t
			= \frac{1}{T}\int_{I_{T}(\tau)}\Gamma(\vec{r};t,t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}t ,
		\end{equation}
		which still depends on where in the window the pair of instants is placed, and the $t$
		integral is a genuine integral: it averages, over the window, correlations that may be
		different at different moments, as they are for a pulse. Stationarity, in the form
		\eqref{eq:GammaTau}, says that $\Gamma(\vec{r};t,t+\tau)=\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)$ for every
		$t$, so the integrand no longer depends on the variable of integration and may be taken
		out, leaving the length \eqref{eq:StripLimits} of the strip,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CTstationary}
			E\left\{{}^{\omega}C_{T}(\vec{r};\tau)\right\} = \frac{1}{T}\,\textcolor{blue}{\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)}\int_{I_{T}(\tau)}\mathrm{d}t
			= \frac{T-|\tau|}{T}\,\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) = \left(1-\frac{|\tau|}{T}\right)\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) .
		\end{equation}
		This is the precise sense in which stationarity simplifies the problem: it collapses the
		two time function to a function of the delay alone and turns the $t$ integral into a
		mere count of how many pairs separated by $\tau$ fit in the window.

		Ergodicity acts on the bracket itself and not on its average. Factoring the length of
		the strip,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CTergodic}
			{}^{\omega}C_{T}(\vec{r};\tau) = \left(1-\frac{|\tau|}{T}\right)\left[\frac{1}{T-|\tau|}\int_{I_{T}(\tau)}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}t\right],
		\end{equation}
		and the new bracket is the time average of $\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)$
		over a window of length $T-|\tau|$ whose two ends run off to $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ as
		$T\to\infty$ (each half of the window is a one sided average, and Birkhoff's theorem
		guarantees that the forward and the backward averages converge to the same limit). By
		\eqref{eq:GammaTimeAverage}, for every fixed $\tau$ and almost every $\omega$,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:CTlimit}
			\lim_{T\to\infty}{}^{\omega}C_{T}(\vec{r};\tau)
			= \underbrace{\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{|\tau|}{T}\right)}_{=\,1}\;\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T-|\tau|}\int_{I_{T}(\tau)}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}t
			= \Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) .
		\end{equation}
		The chain of the three expressions is the content of the argument. Without hypotheses
		the coherence function is a function $\Gamma(\vec{r};t,t')$ of two instants and an
		average over the ensemble; stationarity reduces it to a function $\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)$
		of the delay, so that the window only weighs it with the triangle $1-|\tau|/T$;
		ergodicity identifies that same function with the time integral that a single beam
		accumulates in the laboratory,

		\begin{equation}
			\Gamma(\vec{r};t,t') = E\left\{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(\vec{r},t')\right\}
			\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{stationarity}\;}\;
			\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)
			\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{ergodicity}\;}\;
			\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\omega(\vec{r},t)\,\omega^{*}(\vec{r},t+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}t .
		\end{equation}
		The integral over the ensemble has become an integral over time, and the double integral
		over $t$ and $t'$ has become a single integral over the delay, the integral over the
		remaining instant $t$ having been absorbed either into the length $T-|\tau|$ (on average)
		or into the time average (along one realisation).

		A word of caution is needed before the limit. Equation \eqref{eq:CTlimit} holds for each
		fixed $\tau$, but the periodogram \eqref{eq:PeriodogramCorrelogram} integrates
		${}^{\omega}C_{T}$ up to $|\tau|=T$, where the strip is short and the time average is
		taken over almost nothing; these poorly estimated delays are what keep the periodogram of
		a single realisation from converging, as remarked after \eqref{eq:SpecW}. This is why the
		definition takes the expectation first. Doing so in \eqref{eq:PeriodogramCorrelogram},
		where $E$ passes through the $\tau$ integral by the same Fubini argument, and inserting
		\eqref{eq:CTstationary},

		\begin{equation}
			\frac{E\left\{{}^\omega S_{T}(\vec{r},\nu)\right\}}{T}
			= \int_{-T}^{T}E\left\{{}^{\omega}C_{T}(\vec{r};\tau)\right\}e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau
			= \int_{-T}^{T}\left(1-\frac{|\tau|}{T}\right)\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau .
		\end{equation}
		It remains to let $T\to\infty$. Extending the integrand by zero for $|\tau|>T$, the
		triangular factor tends to one for every fixed $\tau$, and the integrand is bounded in
		modulus by $|\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)|$ uniformly in $T$. If the coherence function is
		integrable, which is the statement that correlations die out after a finite coherence
		time, the dominated convergence theorem allows the limit to be taken inside the integral,
		and

		\begin{equation}
			S(\vec{r},\nu) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau ,
		\end{equation}

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:WienerKhinchin}
			\boxed{S(\vec{r},\nu) = \mathcal{F}\left\{ \Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) \right\}, \qquad \Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}S(\vec{r},\nu)\,e^{i2\pi\nu\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\nu }.
		\end{equation}
		The sign of the exponent is the one dictated by the analytic signal: the field contains
		factors $e^{-i2\pi\nu t}$, so $\mathcal{E}(t)\mathcal{E}^{*}(t+\tau)$ oscillates as
		$e^{+i2\pi\nu\tau}$, and the transform that extracts $S$ carries the opposite sign.

		Result \eqref{eq:WienerKhinchin} reads: the Fourier transform of the coherence function
		is the power spectral density, and it is known as the Wiener-Khinchin theorem. Every step
		of the derivation has a physical reading. The truncation was forced on us because the
		stationary field has infinite energy; the division by $T$ converted energy into power;
		the shear \eqref{eq:ChangeTtau} reorganised the double integral by delays;
		stationarity was used exactly once, in \eqref{eq:CTstationary}, to replace the
		correlation at two instants by $\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)$; ergodicity, in
		\eqref{eq:CTlimit}, guarantees that this $\Gamma$ is the correlation that a single beam
		actually delivers to an interferometer, so that the spectrum we compute is the spectrum
		of the light in the laboratory and not of a fictitious ensemble; and the triangular
		window $1-|\tau|/T$ records that an observation of duration $T$ samples the delay $\tau$
		only during a time $T-|\tau|$, so the measured spectrum converges to the true one once
		$T$ is much longer than the coherence time. Evaluating the inverse relation at $\tau=0$ gives
		$\Gamma(\vec{r};0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}S(\vec{r},\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\nu$: the mean intensity
		is the sum of the powers carried by all frequencies, finite exactly as the energy was
		not. Moreover $S\geq0$, being a limit of expectations of non negative quantities, so the
		Fourier transform of a coherence function can never be negative, which is the spectral
		face of the non negative definiteness \eqref{eq:GammaPositive}.

		Its use is fundamental, since it tells us that by methods such as spectrometry we can find
		the coherence function of any phenomenon, and conversely. This is the principle of Fourier
		transform spectroscopy: a Michelson interferometer measures the visibility of the fringes
		as a function of the path difference, that is, $\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau)$ as a function of
		$\tau$, and a Fourier transform turns that record into the spectrum of the source. We can
		even go further, since this result really stems from something more general, the
		correlation function, meaning that every stationary stochastic phenomenon has an
		associated spectrum, and from the measurements or estimates of that spectrum we can
		compute the correlations in the system.

		If we make a simple observation, the optical range of radiation - the visible - is a
		band of very low frequencies relative to the whole spectrum; the visible runs roughly from
		$400$~nm to $700$~nm, and we may say that all of optics is \textit{narrowband}. This obeys
		the inequality

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:NarrowBand}
			\frac{\triangle \nu}{\nu} \ll 1,
		\end{equation}

		and, in this narrowband regime, the coherence function takes the form

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:NarrowBandGamma}
			\Gamma(\vec{r};\tau) = \tilde{\Gamma}(\vec{r};\tau)\,e^{i2\pi \tilde{\nu}\tau},
		\end{equation}

		which we can represent graphically as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:narrow}, where one can see
		the behaviour of the coherence function in the narrowband regime.\footnote{One usually
		finds in the literature a term similar but not equal to that of narrowband, known as the
		quasi-monochromaticity condition, written as $\frac{\triangle \lambda}{\lambda} \ll 1$; but
		this bounds the wavelength so that it is as monochromatic as possible, while the narrowband
		condition admits several frequencies, provided they are narrow relative to the whole
		frequency spectrum.} There one sees an envelope (the observable) enveloping all the
		fundamental frequencies.

			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Chapter 6/Figures/Estrecho}

These oscillations are confined within something known as the coherence time $\tau_C$, which we shall see later.}

	\subsection{The wave equation of coherence}

		Let us step a little out of the comfort zone and understand how correlations propagate
		through space and time. Let $\mathcal{E}^{(r)}(\vec{r},t)$ be some \emph{real} component
		of the electric field, viewed as a random signal. As is well known from classical
		electromagnetic theory, it obeys Maxwell's equations and therefore has an associated wave
		equation, namely

		\begin{equation}
			\nabla^2 \mathcal{E}^{(r)}(\vec{r},t) = \frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{E}^{(r)}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t^2},
		\end{equation}

		where $C$ is the speed of light in vacuum. We also have, by Fourier analysis,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:EandFourier}
			\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t) = 2\int_0^{\infty} \tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu) e^{-i2\pi\nu t}d\nu .
		\end{equation}

		And if we apply the d'Alembertian operator $\square = \nabla^2 -
		\frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}$ to equation \eqref{eq:EandFourier}, we find
		that $\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu)$ has the form of a Helmholtz-type equation, that is,

		\begin{equation}
			\nabla^2 \mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t) - \frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t^2} = 2\int_0^{\infty} \left[ \nabla^2 \tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu) + k^2 \tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu) \right]e^{-i2\pi\nu t}d\nu,
		\end{equation}

		which leads us to the fact that the electric field, as an analytic and complex signal, also
		obeys a wave-type equation

		\begin{equation}
			\nabla^2 \mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t) = \frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t^2}.
		\end{equation}

		Taking the complex conjugate of the previous equation we have

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:NearlyWaveForE}
			\nabla^2 \mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r},t) = \frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t^2}.
		\end{equation}

		If we multiply both sides of equation \eqref{eq:NearlyWaveForE} by
		$\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',t')$, and since we have taken the d'Alembertian with respect to the
		unprimed variables, using some sign rules we have

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:NoAvg}
			\nabla^2 \left[ \mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r},t)\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',t') \right] =  \frac{1}{C^2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\left[ \mathcal{E}^*(\vec{r},t)\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}',t') \right].
		\end{equation}

		Taking the ensemble average of equation \eqref{eq:NoAvg} over all realisations of the
		field, this wave equation becomes

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:coherenceWave1}
			\nabla^2 \Gamma(\vec{r},\vec{r}';t,t') = \frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} \Gamma(\vec{r},\vec{r}';t,t'),
		\end{equation}

		and, similarly, using $\square' = \nabla'^2 - \frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t'^2}$
		we have another wave equation given by

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:coherenceWave2}
			\nabla'^2 \Gamma(\vec{r},\vec{r}';t,t') = \frac{1}{C^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t'^2} \Gamma(\vec{r},\vec{r}';t,t').
		\end{equation}

		Equations \eqref{eq:coherenceWave1} and \eqref{eq:coherenceWave2} tell us directly that the
		coherence functions between two spacetime points evolve in time following a wave-type
		equation, separately, with respect to each pair of spacetime coordinates - a result
		that is, without doubt, remarkable. We can take this result even further and study this
		propagation in the next section.

	\subsection{Propagation of coherence and the direction of time: the van Cittert-Zernike theorem}

		\begin{figure}[htbp]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Chapter 6/Figures/Propagation}
			\caption{Propagation of the electric field from a plane $Z=0$ to a plane $Z$, where each point of the wavefront carries its mean polarisation ellipse.}
			\label{fig:propagation}
		\end{figure}

		The problem solved by van Cittert and Zernike is the following. On the plane $z=0$ there
		is a source, or an aperture illuminated by one, whose coherence function
		$\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,';\tau)$ we know, and we want to find the coherence function
		$\Gamma(\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2};\tau)$ on a plane placed at a distance $z$, as illustrated
		in Figure~\ref{fig:propagation}. The way to do it is already contained in the choice
		\eqref{eq:FieldSampleSpace} of the sample space: every realisation $\omega$ is a solution
		of Maxwell's equations, randomness enters only through the source, and therefore each
		realisation propagates exactly as a deterministic field does in the diffraction chapter
		(see Section~\ref{Sec:Diffraction}). We propagate the realisations and then average, and
		the statistics are simply carried along by the propagation. As in the definition
		\eqref{eq:CoherenceFunction} we work with a single Cartesian component $\mathcal{E}$ of
		the analytic signal, so the treatment is scalar: at each point of the wavefront that
		component oscillates randomly, but within the mean polarisation ellipse of its
		representative state.

		We start from one spectral component. For the frequency $\nu$, with wavenumber
		$k=2\pi\nu/C$ and wavelength $\lambda=C/\nu$, the Huygens-Fresnel principle
		\eqref{eq:RSfinal} gives the field at an observation point $\vec{r}_{1}$ as a sum of
		spherical wavelets emitted by the points $\vec{r}$ of the plane $\Sigma$ of the source,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:VCZspectral}
			\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r}_{1},\nu) = \frac{1}{i\lambda}\int_{\Sigma}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu)\,\frac{e^{ikR_{1}}}{R_{1}}\,\cos\theta_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,
			\qquad R_{1} = \|\vec{r}_{1}-\vec{r}\|, \qquad \cos\theta_{1} = \hat{z}\cdot\frac{\vec{r}_{1}-\vec{r}}{R_{1}},
		\end{equation}
		where, exactly as in \eqref{eq:RSobliquity}, the obliquity angle is measured from the
		vector that goes from the source point to the observation point. Strictly speaking, by
		the theorem proved in the previous subsection a stationary realisation has no Fourier
		transform; \eqref{eq:VCZspectral} must be read for the truncated realisation
		\eqref{eq:NearlyWiener}, with a window much longer than any propagation time involved.
		This is harmless, because the result we are about to reach is written entirely in the
		time domain and no longer contains any Fourier transform.

		To return to the time domain we insert \eqref{eq:VCZspectral} into the analytic signal
		\eqref{eq:AnalyticSignal}, write $1/\lambda=\nu/C$ and $kR_{1}=2\pi\nu R_{1}/C$, and
		collect the two exponentials,

		\begin{align}
			\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}_{1},t) &= 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r}_{1},\nu)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu
			= 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\textcolor{blue}{\nu}}{i\textcolor{blue}{C}}\int_{\Sigma}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu)\,\frac{e^{\textcolor{blue}{i2\pi\nu R_{1}/C}}}{R_{1}}\,\cos\theta_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\;e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu \nonumber\\
			&= \frac{1}{iC}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\cos\theta_{1}}{R_{1}}\left[2\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu\,\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu\left(t-R_{1}/C\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right]\mathrm{d}\sigma , \label{eq:VCZtimeExact}
		\end{align}
		where the order of the two integrations was exchanged, since the source occupies a
		bounded region and the spectrum a finite band. The bracket would be the analytic signal
		at the source evaluated at the instant $t-R_{1}/C$, were it not for the factor $\nu$.
		That factor is what turns a field into its time derivative, because
		$\partial_{t}e^{-i2\pi\nu t}=-i2\pi\nu\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}$, so that the bracket equals
		$\frac{i}{2\pi}\,\dot{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},t-R_{1}/C)$ and \eqref{eq:VCZtimeExact} is the
		exact statement

		\begin{equation}
			\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}_{1},t) = \frac{1}{2\pi C}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial t}\left(\vec{r},\,t-\frac{R_{1}}{C}\right)\frac{\cos\theta_{1}}{R_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma .
		\end{equation}
		Two features of this expression carry the physics. The first is causality: the field at
		$\vec{r}_{1}$ at the instant $t$ is built from what the source did at the
		\textit{retarded} instant $t-R_{1}/C$, the moment at which the light that arrives now left
		the point $\vec{r}$. The second is that the source enters through its time derivative,
		which is the time domain face of the factor $1/\lambda$ of the Huygens-Fresnel principle:
		higher frequencies radiate more efficiently.

		For narrowband light \eqref{eq:NarrowBand} the spectrum $\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{r},\nu)$
		is concentrated in a band of width $\triangle\nu\ll\tilde{\nu}$ about the central
		frequency, so inside the bracket $\nu$ may be replaced by $\tilde{\nu}$, with a relative
		error of the order of $\triangle\nu/\tilde{\nu}$, and taken out of the integral. What
		remains is $\tilde{\nu}\,\mathcal{E}(\vec{r},t-R_{1}/C)$, and with the mean wavelength
		$\tilde{\lambda}=C/\tilde{\nu}$,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:IntegralEz}
			\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}_{1},t) = \frac{1}{i\tilde{\lambda}}\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{E}\left(\vec{r},\,t-\frac{R_{1}}{C}\right)\frac{\cos\theta_{1}}{R_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma .
		\end{equation}
		Notice that the approximation touches only the amplitude factor $\nu$; the phase
		$2\pi\nu R_{1}/C$, which is enormous, has been kept exactly in the form of the retarded
		time.

		We can visualise graphically how we intend to propagate in Figure~\ref{fig:propagation2}:
		we start from the coherence function between two points $\vec{r},\vec{r}\,'$ of the source
		and carry it to the coherence function between two points $\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2}$ of the
		observation plane.

		\begin{figure}[htbp]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Chapter 6/Figures/Propagation2}
			\caption{Propagation of the coherence function between two points $\vec{r},\vec{r}'$ on the initial plane to the coherence between two points $\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2$ on the observation plane.}
			\label{fig:propagation2}
		\end{figure}

		With the convention used throughout this chapter, \eqref{eq:CoherenceFunction} with
		$t'=t+\tau$, the coherence function on the observation plane is
		$\Gamma(\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2};\tau)=E\{\mathcal{E}(\vec{r}_{1},t)\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(\vec{r}_{2},t+\tau)\}$.
		We insert \eqref{eq:IntegralEz} for each of the two factors, using an independent source
		point $\vec{r}\,'$, with $R_{2}=\|\vec{r}_{2}-\vec{r}\,'\|$ and
		$\cos\theta_{2}=\hat{z}\cdot(\vec{r}_{2}-\vec{r}\,')/R_{2}$, for the second, which is
		evaluated at the instant $t+\tau$ and conjugated,

		\begin{align}
			\Gamma(\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2};\tau) &= E\left\{\frac{1}{i\tilde{\lambda}}\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{E}\left(\vec{r},\,t-\frac{R_{1}}{C}\right)\frac{\cos\theta_{1}}{R_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\cdot\;\frac{1}{-i\tilde{\lambda}}\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{E}^{*}\left(\vec{r}\,',\,t+\tau-\frac{R_{2}}{C}\right)\frac{\cos\theta_{2}}{R_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma'\right\} \nonumber\\
			&= \frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}\int_{\Sigma}E\left\{\mathcal{E}\left(\vec{r},\,t-\frac{R_{1}}{C}\right)\mathcal{E}^{*}\left(\vec{r}\,',\,t+\tau-\frac{R_{2}}{C}\right)\right\}\frac{\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}}{R_{1}R_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}\sigma' ,
		\end{align}
		where $\frac{1}{i\tilde{\lambda}}\cdot\frac{1}{-i\tilde{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{-i^{2}\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}$ and where the
		expectation has been carried inside the two surface integrals by Fubini's theorem, which
		is legitimate because by \eqref{eq:CauchySchwarzProb} and \eqref{eq:FiniteEnergy} the
		modulus of the integrand is bounded in mean by
		$\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r};0)^{1/2}\,\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r}\,';0)^{1/2}\,/(R_{1}R_{2})$, which is
		integrable over a bounded source. The expectation that remains is the coherence function
		of the source between $(\vec{r},t-R_{1}/C)$ and $(\vec{r}\,',t+\tau-R_{2}/C)$, and since
		the source is stationary it depends only on the difference of the two instants,

		\begin{equation}
			\left(t+\tau-\frac{R_{2}}{C}\right)-\left(t-\frac{R_{1}}{C}\right) = \cancel{t}+\tau-\frac{R_{2}}{C}-\cancel{t}+\frac{R_{1}}{C} = \tau+\frac{R_{1}-R_{2}}{C},
		\end{equation}
		so that

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:IntGamma}
			\Gamma(\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2};\tau) = \frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}\int_{\Sigma}\Gamma_{0}\left(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,';\,\tau+\frac{R_{1}-R_{2}}{C}\right)\frac{\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}}{R_{1}R_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}\sigma' .
		\end{equation}
		The instant $t$ has disappeared, which says that \textbf{stationarity is preserved by
		propagation}: the light on the observation plane is again stationary, and its coherence
		function depends on $\tau$ alone. Each pair of source points contributes its own
		correlation, but evaluated at a delay corrected by the \emph{difference} of the two
		travel times, $(R_{1}-R_{2})/C$, and not by their sum: what the observation compares is
		the light emitted by $\vec{r}$ a time $R_{1}/C$ ago with the light emitted by
		$\vec{r}\,'$ a time $R_{2}/C$ ago, so only the relative retardation matters.

		\begin{figure}[htbp]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{Chapter 6/Figures/Random2}
			\caption{Evolution of the realisations upon propagating the electric field.}
			\label{fig:random2}
		\end{figure}

		The propagation of the random oscillations across $n$ realisations can be visualised in
		Figure~\ref{fig:random2}.

		We now specialise to the case that gives the theorem its interest, a thermal source such
		as a lamp, a star or the Sun, in which every atom radiates independently of its
		neighbours. The field at two distinct points of such a source is uncorrelated as soon as
		the points are farther apart than a distance of the order of $\tilde{\lambda}$, and since
		no detail finer than a wavelength survives propagation over distances much larger than
		$\tilde{\lambda}$, the correlation may be idealised as a delta,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:IncoherentSource}
			\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,';\tau) = \kappa\,\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r};\tau)\,\delta(\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,') .
		\end{equation}
		Here $\delta$ is the two dimensional delta on the plane $\Sigma$, which has dimensions of
		an inverse area, and the constant $\kappa$, which has dimensions of area, is required so
		that both sides of \eqref{eq:IncoherentSource} have the dimensions of an intensity; it
		measures the small area over which the source is still correlated, and a more careful
		treatment gives $\kappa=\tilde{\lambda}^{2}/\pi$ \citep{goodman2015statistical}. The weight
		$\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r};\tau)$ is the mutual coherence function \eqref{eq:SelfCoherence} at
		the source point. A field that is literally delta correlated could not exist, since it
		would carry infinite power at infinitely fine scales, but this does not matter: we shall
		see that $\kappa$ drops out of every normalised quantity. Inserting
		\eqref{eq:IncoherentSource} into \eqref{eq:IntGamma}, the delta collapses the integral over
		$\vec{r}\,'$ and sets $\vec{r}\,'=\vec{r}$ everywhere, in particular in
		$R_{2}=\|\vec{r}_{2}-\vec{r}\|$ and in $\cos\theta_{2}$,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:VCZincoherent}
			\Gamma(\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2};\tau) = \frac{\kappa}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}\Gamma_{0}\left(\vec{r};\,\tau+\frac{R_{1}-R_{2}}{C}\right)\frac{\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}}{R_{1}R_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,
			\qquad R_{1}=\|\vec{r}_{1}-\vec{r}\|,\quad R_{2}=\|\vec{r}_{2}-\vec{r}\|.
		\end{equation}
		The two observation points are now illuminated by the \emph{same} source points, and this
		is the whole mechanism of the theorem: although no two points of the source are
		correlated, every point of the source sends light to both $\vec{r}_{1}$ and
		$\vec{r}_{2}$, and the common origin of that light correlates them.

		In nature we will not always have narrowband sources; however, we can resort to another
		argument, namely that most instruments do not have an infinite or broad range but a short
		one, or, in other words, measurement instruments measure a narrow part of the spectrum.
		We therefore write the coherence function of the source in the narrowband form
		\eqref{eq:NarrowBandGamma},
		$\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r};\tau)=\tilde{\Gamma}_{0}(\vec{r};\tau)\,e^{i2\pi\tilde{\nu}\tau}$, an
		envelope that varies slowly, on the scale of the coherence time
		$\tau_{C}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\gamma(\vec{r};\tau)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau$
		\citep{goodman2015statistical}, times a carrier that oscillates at the central frequency.
		At the delay that appears in \eqref{eq:VCZincoherent},

		\begin{equation}
			\Gamma_{0}\left(\vec{r};\,\tau+\frac{R_{1}-R_{2}}{C}\right) = \tilde{\Gamma}_{0}\left(\vec{r};\,\tau+\frac{R_{1}-R_{2}}{C}\right)e^{i2\pi\tilde{\nu}\tau}\,e^{i2\pi\tilde{\nu}\frac{R_{1}-R_{2}}{C}} .
		\end{equation}
		If the delay is small compared with the coherence time, $|\tau|\ll\tau_{C}$, and the path
		difference is small compared with the coherence length, $|R_{1}-R_{2}|\ll C\tau_{C}$,
		the envelope has no time to change and may be evaluated at zero delay,
		$\tilde{\Gamma}_{0}(\vec{r};0)=\Gamma_{0}(\vec{r};0)=I_{0}(\vec{r})$, which by property
		a) is the mean intensity of the source at $\vec{r}$. The carrier, on the other hand, may
		not be dropped, because $2\pi\tilde{\nu}(R_{1}-R_{2})/C=\tilde{k}(R_{1}-R_{2})$, with
		$\tilde{k}=2\pi/\tilde{\lambda}$, is a phase of many radians even for tiny path
		differences. The factor $e^{i2\pi\tilde{\nu}\tau}$ is common to the whole integral, and
		it is the trivial oscillation of the propagated coherence function at the central
		frequency; all the information is in what multiplies it, so we evaluate at $\tau=0$ and
		obtain

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:VCZgeneral}
			\Gamma(\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2};0) = \frac{\kappa}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}I_{0}(\vec{r})\,e^{i\tilde{k}\left(R_{1}-R_{2}\right)}\frac{\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}}{R_{1}R_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma .
		\end{equation}
		This is already the van Cittert-Zernike theorem in its general form. Its structure is
		that of the Huygens-Fresnel integral \eqref{eq:RSfinal}, with the intensity of the source
		playing the role of the field on the aperture, and this is what is meant by saying that
		\textit{coherence diffracts}.

		To make the result transparent we place ourselves in the paraxial regime of the
		diffraction chapter: the source and the observation region are small compared with the
		distance $z$, so that $\cos\theta_{1}\approx\cos\theta_{2}\approx1$ and, in the amplitude,
		$R_{1}R_{2}\approx z^{2}$, while in the phase, multiplied by the enormous $\tilde{k}$, the
		quadratic term must be retained, exactly as in \eqref{aprox_r}. With $\vec{r}=(x,y,0)$ on
		the source and $\vec{r}_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j},z)$ on the observation plane,

		\begin{align}
			R_{1} &= \left(z^{2}+(x_{1}-x)^{2}+(y_{1}-y)^{2}\right)^{1/2} = z\left(1+\frac{(x_{1}-x)^{2}+(y_{1}-y)^{2}}{z^{2}}\right)^{1/2} \nonumber\\
			&\approx z+\frac{(x_{1}-x)^{2}+(y_{1}-y)^{2}}{2z},\\
			R_{2} &\approx z+\frac{(x_{2}-x)^{2}+(y_{2}-y)^{2}}{2z}.
		\end{align}
		Expanding the squares, $(x_{j}-x)^{2}=x_{j}^{2}-2xx_{j}+x^{2}$, and subtracting,

		\begin{align}
			R_{1}-R_{2} &\approx \cancel{z}-\cancel{z}+\frac{x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}-2(xx_{1}+yy_{1})+\cancel{x^{2}+y^{2}}-x_{2}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}+2(xx_{2}+yy_{2})-\cancel{x^{2}-y^{2}}}{2z} \nonumber\\
			&= \frac{\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}\right)-\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)}{2z}-\frac{x(x_{1}-x_{2})+y(y_{1}-y_{2})}{z}.
		\end{align}
		Let us define the transverse vectors $\vec{S}=(x,y)$ on the source and
		$\vec{S}_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1})$, $\vec{S}_{2}=(x_{2},y_{2})$ on the observation plane. Since
		$\tilde{k}/2z=\pi/\tilde{\lambda}z$ and $\tilde{k}/z=2\pi/\tilde{\lambda}z$, the phase of
		\eqref{eq:VCZgeneral} is

		\begin{equation}
			\tilde{k}\left(R_{1}-R_{2}\right) \approx \underbrace{\frac{\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}z}\left(\|\vec{S}_{1}\|^{2}-\|\vec{S}_{2}\|^{2}\right)}_{\equiv\,\psi(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{2})}-\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}z}\,\vec{S}\cdot\left(\vec{S}_{1}-\vec{S}_{2}\right),
		\end{equation}
		and, since $I_{0}$ vanishes outside the source, the integral may be extended to the whole
		plane,

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:VanCittertZernike}
			\boxed{\Gamma(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{2};0) = \frac{\kappa\,e^{i\psi(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{2})}}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}I_{0}(\vec{S})\,e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}z}\vec{S}\cdot\left(\vec{S}_{1}-\vec{S}_{2}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}S }.
		\end{equation}
		Up to the phase factor $e^{i\psi}$, the coherence between two points of the observation
		plane is the two dimensional Fourier transform of the intensity of the source, evaluated
		at the spatial frequency $(\vec{S}_{1}-\vec{S}_{2})/\tilde{\lambda}z$, with the same sign
		convention as the Fraunhofer integral \eqref{eq:FresnelThreeFactors}. Two remarks make
		the content precise. The first is that the quadratic phase in the source coordinates,
		$x^{2}+y^{2}$, has cancelled exactly, because both distances are measured from the same
		source point; for this reason \eqref{eq:VanCittertZernike} requires only the paraxial
		approximation, and not the far field condition that the Fraunhofer formula of diffraction
		demands. The second is that $\kappa$ disappears from the normalised quantity. The
		intensity on the observation plane is
		$I(\vec{S}_{1})=\Gamma(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{1};0)=\kappa\int I_{0}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}S/\tilde{\lambda}^{2}z^{2}$,
		since $\psi(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{1})=0$, so the complex degree of coherence between the
		two observation points is

		\begin{equation}\label{eq:VCZdegree}
			\gamma(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{2}) = \frac{\Gamma(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{2};0)}{\sqrt{I(\vec{S}_{1})\,I(\vec{S}_{2})}}
			= e^{i\psi(\vec{S}_{1},\vec{S}_{2})}\,\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}I_{0}(\vec{S})\,e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}z}\vec{S}\cdot\left(\vec{S}_{1}-\vec{S}_{2}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}S}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}I_{0}(\vec{S})\,\mathrm{d}^{2}S},
		\end{equation}
		which contains no trace of $\kappa$ and whose modulus depends only on the separation
		$\vec{S}_{1}-\vec{S}_{2}$ of the two points. Taking the reference point on the axis,
		$\vec{S}_{2}=\vec{0}$, the coherence of every other point with it is the Fourier
		integral of the intensity, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fouriergamma}.

		\begin{figure}[htbp]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.65\linewidth]{Chapter 6/Figures/FourierGamma}
			\caption{The mutual coherence propagated to the observation plane is the Fourier transform of the intensity distribution $I_0(\vec{S})$ of the source.}
			\label{fig:fouriergamma}
		\end{figure}

		The physical consequence is immediate from the reciprocity of Fourier widths. A source of
		transverse size $D$ has an intensity whose transform has a width of the order of
		$\tilde{\lambda}z/D$, so the light it produces at a distance $z$ is coherent over a
		transverse distance $\ell_{C}\sim\tilde{\lambda}z/D=\tilde{\lambda}/\alpha$, where
		$\alpha=D/z$ is the angle subtended by the source. The smaller the source looks, the
		larger the patch over which its light is coherent. For the Sun, with
		$\alpha\approx9.3\times10^{-3}$~rad and $\tilde{\lambda}\approx550$~nm, this gives a
		coherence width of some tens of micrometres, which is why Young's experiment with
		sunlight needs pinholes very close together; for a star, whose angular size is smaller by
		a factor of the order of $10^{5}$, it amounts to metres. Michelson and Pease turned the
		argument around in 1920: measuring the separation of two mirrors at which the fringes of
		Betelgeuse disappeared, they read off $|\gamma|$, inverted \eqref{eq:VCZdegree}, and
		obtained the first measurement of the angular diameter of a star other than the Sun
		\citep{michelson1921diameter}.

		How can we tell in which direction time flows? This may sound like a question rather out of
		context for these notes, but if we could travel back to the golden age of Boltzmann, we
		would find a heated debate of the time on how statistical physics gives a direction to
		time, an \textit{arrow}. Paraphrasing Boltzmann, it is said that \textit{time advances in
		the direction in which correlations increase}. If we look at the van Cittert-Zernike
		theorem, we can start from a source whose points are completely uncorrelated but, as its light
		propagates, it gradually becomes correlated; time grows and so does coherence, until a point is reached at
		which its radiation is coherent. This is, without doubt, one of the
		strongest and most beautiful results we can find in statistical physics.
